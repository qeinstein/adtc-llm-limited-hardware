============================================================
DENSE -> SPARSE -> STORAGE-RESIDENT LLM LITERATURE NOTEBOOK
============================================================

LAST UPDATED: 2026-09-12
CURRENT RESEARCH QUESTION:

Can a large pretrained dense model, with Qwen3.8-27B as the main candidate,
be transformed into a conditionally executed sparse model whose inactive
parameters remain on SSD/flash, while retaining most capability on a
commodity Linux/x86 CPU with less than 8 GB RAM?  The target is single-user
autoregressive decode, eventually using llama.cpp/GGUF, where actual wall
clock tokens/second matters more than FLOP reduction.

RESEARCH SCOPE / EVIDENCE DISCIPLINE:

This notebook deliberately distinguishes:

1. STATIC PRUNING: permanently remove weights.
2. ACTIVATION SPARSITY: many neurons naturally produce zero or small values.
3. CONTEXTUAL SPARSITY: the active parameter subset depends on input context.
4. MIXTURE OF EXPERTS: an explicitly trained router selects parameter groups.
5. MOEFICATION: post-hoc functional partition of a dense FFN.
6. SPARSE UPCYCLING: initialize a new MoE from a dense checkpoint and continue
   training.
7. STORAGE RESIDENCY: inactive weights still exist but are outside RAM.

Numbers are tagged with model, hardware, batch, precision, context/decode
conditions where the source reports them. A FLOP claim is not treated as a
wall-clock claim. “Not reported” is intentional where a source or public
repository does not expose the required measurement.

Evidence status: 18 source records are included below. The source set contains
papers, implementation repositories, primary model/configuration files, and a
storage-energy analysis. Edge0, DejaVu and PowerInfer code were inspected from
the repositories; Edge0 was inspected at commit 03a5a15, DejaVu at ee902c1.
The notebook is a research artifact, not an implementation plan approval.

------------------------------------------------------------
1. EXECUTIVE SYNTHESIS
------------------------------------------------------------

CURRENT STATE OF THE EVIDENCE

Prior art establishes each major ingredient separately, and recent work has
now combined most of them:

- MoEfication shows that a dense FFN can be partitioned into functionally
  aligned neuron experts and selected at runtime without changing the total
  dense parameter count. It is primarily a ReLU-era method and does not by
  itself solve modern gated FFNs or SSD I/O.
- DejaVu shows that an already dense model has contextual head and MLP
  sparsity and that shallow predictors can choose sparse structures before
  the expensive operation. It reports GPU wall-clock speedups, not an SSD
  resident CPU design.
- LLM in a Flash, PowerInfer and PowerInfer-2 show that storage bandwidth,
  read granularity, neuron bundling, hot/cold placement, caches and overlap
  can matter more than nominal arithmetic. Their device and activation
  assumptions differ from Linux/x86 NVMe.
- Edge0 demonstrates a practical SSD-resident sparse-MoE runtime with
  mmap-backed expert bundles, global LRU, prefetch buffers, fixed staging
  slots, asynchronous cross-token/cross-layer routing prediction and
  adapter recovery. It starts from a sparse MoE checkpoint; it does not
  demonstrate dense Qwen3.8 -> sparse conversion.
- DynaMoE demonstrates post-training conversion of dense MLPs into nested
  variable-width experts with a router and modest continued fine-tuning. It
  has no real storage-resident benchmark and its quality drops are material
  at aggressive budgets.
- NeuroPrefetcher is the closest recent system analogue: it predicts future
  sparse masks, stores neuron records in a storage-friendly layout, reads
  only mask deltas, reuses fixed slots, and overlaps NVMe with execution.
  Its evaluation is Jetson GPU/Arm/unified-memory, not x86 CPU, and it still
  achieves only roughly 1.7-3.7 tok/s under 9-13.2 GiB caps on its reported
  workload. This substantially narrows any claim that delta prefetching or
  neuron-centric SSD layout is new by itself.

WHAT PRIOR ART ALREADY ESTABLISHES

- A bounded resident working set plus a larger storage-resident model is
  physically possible. The hardest issue is the bytes and latency of the
  changing working set, not mmap syntax.
- SSD reads need large, coalesced, reuse-heavy transfers. Small random reads
  can erase the benefit of sparsity. A practical design must store aligned
  gate/up/down neuron or group records and maintain fixed staging slots.
- Prediction must happen early enough to hide storage. A predictor launched
  immediately before an FFN is usually too late if that FFN has to be read
  from SSD. Cross-layer or cross-token prediction is the useful pattern.
- A gated FFN must preserve the shared intermediate index: the same neuron
  row in gate and up and the corresponding column in down. Loading one
  projection without the aligned others is incorrect.
- The storage system needs a cache policy, explicit prefetch queue, bounded
  staging buffers, a miss/fallback policy and statistics. mmap alone is not a
  storage scheduler.
- A dense model can expose substantial contextual sparsity, but modern SiLU/
  SwiGLU is less naturally sparse than ReLU. A paper reporting 90% ReLU
  sparsity is not evidence for 90% safe Qwen3.8 SwiGLU sparsity.
- Reducing active FFN work does not make all model compute disappear. Qwen3.8
  has 48 linear-attention/hybrid blocks, 16 full-attention blocks, embedding,
  normalization and output-head costs that remain unless separately changed.

WHAT REMAINS UNSOLVED FOR THIS TARGET

- No source found demonstrates a dense Qwen3.8-27B checkpoint transformed into
  a gated, group-sparse, SSD-resident model on a Linux/x86 commodity CPU,
  inside 8 GB RAM, with 10-20 single-stream decode tok/s and measured quality
  retention.
- There is no reliable public estimate of the safe active-neuron fraction for
  the exact Qwen3.8-27B gated DeltaNet/full-attention hybrid. This must be
  measured, not borrowed from OPT, ReLU, sparse Qwen2, or native MoE models.
- The CPU cost of gathering/repacking quantized Qwen rows, dequantizing small
  groups, applying the gate/up product, and writing a down projection is not
  established. Sparse matmul overhead can dominate after the arithmetic is
  reduced.
- A predictor must be judged against storage bytes/token, false negatives and
  cache misses, not only mask accuracy. A false negative may require an
  emergency read or cause a quality error; a false positive wastes SSD and
  bandwidth.
- Under 8 GB, a dense resident prefix, embeddings/output head, attention/
  linear blocks, predictors, KV cache and staging/cache memory may leave too
  little room for useful active MLP rows. Exact accounting is needed.

STRONGEST EVIDENCE THE IDEA CAN WORK

- Edge0 reports 2.9 GiB peak active memory for an SSD-offloaded 35B-class
  sparse model and 14.9-17.7 tok/s decode on an M4 Pro, with 4-bit expert
  bundles and prerouting. This validates the systems pattern, although not
  the target model, CPU or conversion method.
- LLM in a Flash reports 669 ms/token-equivalent end-to-end latency for OPT
  6.7B on M1 CPU using selective persistence, prediction, windows and
  bundling, versus 3182 ms for a naive baseline in its single-stream setup.
- DejaVu reports 1.8-2x over FasterTransformer on 8 A100 GPUs at structured
  sparsity, with no measured accuracy loss for its tested OPT workloads.
- PowerInfer-2 reports a 47B Mixtral-family model at 11.68 tok/s on a phone
  at a high memory budget, and 2.13 tok/s at a 7 GB memory budget. Its
  cluster/cache/pipeline ablations show that cache hit rate and overlap, not
  arithmetic alone, produce the gain.
- NeuroPrefetcher reports 82-85% token-to-token active-neuron persistence and
  103 MiB/token NVMe reads at a 14.8 GiB cap in its Jetson setup. Delta
  reads and fixed slots are therefore credible design tools, even though
  the absolute throughput target is not yet met.

STRONGEST EVIDENCE IT MAY FAIL

- A 4 GB/s effective SSD budget provides only 400 MB/token at 10 tok/s, or
  200 MB/token at 20 tok/s, before other I/O, queueing, filesystem and
  overlap losses. At realistic random-read rates the budget can be far lower.
- Qwen3.8 has approximately 17.1B MLP parameters. At 4-bit storage the MLP
  pool is roughly 9.6 GB including a rough group-scale overhead, so even 10%
  fresh reads can be about 1 GB per token if reuse is poor.
- Modern SiLU/SwiGLU does not zero the up branch, and it generally has less
  exact activation sparsity than ReLU. Thresholding small contributions can
  change the output continuously rather than merely skipping exact zeros.
- For an 8 GB machine, the non-MLP model, output head, KV cache, runtime,
  predictor and staging buffers may consume most of RAM. If the resident
  floor is 5-7 GB, the active SSD working set becomes very small.
- Published sparse systems mostly use GPUs, NPUs, Apple unified memory or
  native sparse MoE checkpoints. Their kernels and memory systems do not
  prove a CPU/GGUF design.
- SSD offload can be energy-expensive: one 2025 analysis estimates up to
  roughly 12x the per-token energy of HBM for SSD-resident MoE weights. This
  is not a throughput kill by itself, but it matters for sustained deployment
  and flash endurance.

LIKELY ARCHITECTURE

The most plausible path is a hybrid, not a pure new MoE:

1. Keep embeddings, output head, all norms, routers/predictors, linear
   attention and full-attention projections resident where possible.
2. Treat the three Qwen3.8 MLP matrices as aligned intermediate-neuron
   records. Build groups large enough for sequential/coalesced SSD access,
   probably 128-512 intermediate neurons rather than single neurons.
3. Use a dense calibration trace to rank the actual gated contribution
   `|silu(gate_i) * up_i|^2 * ||down_i||^2`, and measure group-level error and
   token-to-token persistence.
4. Start with a training-free or lightly trained lookahead predictor. A
   low-rank/SVD gate predictor is attractive for a no-large-training path;
   a DejaVu/DynaMoE-style learned predictor may recover more quality.
5. Store group records in a GGUF-compatible or adjacent file with aligned,
   coalesced gate/up/down data. Maintain fixed staging slots and read only
   predicted-set deltas. Keep a resident hot core and use a bounded LRU for
   cold groups.
6. Add distillation/fine-tuning only after exact activation traces establish
   a viable sparsity/bytes envelope. Do not begin by building a full MoE
   router and assuming it will preserve the dense function.

LIKELY BOTTLENECK

The limiting resource is the maximum number of fresh, non-reused quantized
bytes that must be delivered per decoded token, multiplied by the latency and
CPU overhead of small/random reads and sparse kernels. The main mathematical
compute may become secondary. A second likely bottleneck is the resident
floor: Qwen3.8's attention/linear/hybrid path and output head may leave no
useful RAM for a large dynamic MLP cache under 8 GB.

POSSIBLE NOVELTY

The core ideas—contextual sparsity, neuron grouping, prediction, SSD
streaming, delta prefetch, hot/cold caches and fixed staging—are already in
the literature. The plausible contribution is an empirically validated
combination for a dense Qwen3.8-style gated hybrid model on Linux/x86,
including a post-hoc conversion, CPU/GGUF-oriented quantized layout, exact
bytes/token and quality/latency tradeoffs under an 8 GB cap. This is a
combination claim only; novelty must not be claimed until a more exhaustive
search and a direct comparison are completed.

[S01] EDGE0

TITLE: Edge0: Streaming Mixture-of-Experts Inference at the Edge
AUTHORS: Edge0-AI repository authors
YEAR: 2025 repository snapshot inspected; README does not establish a peer-
      reviewed publication year
VENUE: Open-source implementation
URL: https://github.com/Edge0-AI/Edge0
CODE: Repository, commit 03a5a15; docs/architecture.md,
      docs/streaming.md, docs/prerouter.md, streaming/{mmap,cache,layer}.py,
      engine/{hooks,qwen}.py, prerouter/{heads,install}.py
RELEVANCE: high

PROBLEM:
Run sparse MoE models whose expert weights exceed practical DRAM without
loading the whole expert pool, while preserving useful decode throughput.

KEY IDEA:
Keep the non-expert model resident, mmap quantized expert bundles from SSD,
reuse a global bounded cache, stage predicted experts into fixed slots, and
overlap prefetch with model execution. Recover some quantization/sparsity
quality loss with LoRA adapters. A learned prerouter predicts the next layer
and next token's expert set before it is needed.

ARCHITECTURE:
The public configurations are Qwen3.5-MoE 35B-A3B (40 layers, 256 routed
experts, top-4 plus one shared expert) and Ling 3.0 8B (24 layers, 128
experts, top-8). The README describes 4-bit weights, approximately 23 GB
and 4.2 GB disk footprints, and claimed peak active memory of about 2.9 GB
and 1.0 GB respectively. The backend in the inspected repository is MLX on
Apple Silicon, not Linux/x86 CUDA or llama.cpp.

ALGORITHM:
Each expert bundle contains gate/up/down weight, scale and bias ranges. The
Qwen gated computation is `down(silu(gate(x)) * up(x))`. The bundle order is
important: fused gate/up has gate rows followed by up rows; using the wrong
order produces a large numerical error.

The mmap reader opens each safetensors shard with `mmap.ACCESS_READ`, parses
the header and returns zero-copy byte slices. `madvise(MADV_WILLNEED)` and a
sequential walk are available, but the public code does not use MAP_POPULATE
and does not guarantee residency.

`SharedExpertCache` is a global OrderedDict LRU keyed by `(layer, expert)`;
this is not one full cache per layer. `PrefetchBuffer` is a separate,
capacity-bounded OrderedDict. A request searches pinned/hot data, shared LRU,
prefetch entries and in-flight futures before building a bundle from its nine
quantized ranges. Thread pools perform load and prefetch work.

The staged path maintains fixed tensor slots and an expert-to-slot table.
Survivor experts remain in their slots, changed experts overwrite slots, and
an overflow slot can represent a missing expert as zero/drop. GPU-side slot
selection avoids host synchronization. `staged_sync` is needed when the
actual used set is reconciled; `staged_replace` can use predicted indices as
the execution set and the production prerouter mapping is intended to avoid
drops.

The prerouter is cross-token and cross-layer. A head owned by layer N reads
that layer's MoE input and predicts layer N+1. Layer N+1 consumes the
prediction made at token t-1, producing one-layer/one-token lookahead. Its
input concatenates the hidden state and current/previous top-k one-hot route
features. A two-layer head is used, with hidden width 512, fp16 by default.
The implementation falls back to the true router for initial/uncovered
layers/tokens.

IMPORTANT EQUATIONS:
Gated MLP:
`y = down(silu(gate(x)) * up(x))`.

Quantized affine group of 64 (conceptually):
`w ~= scale * q + bias`, with packed 4-bit values. The inspected layout
stores weight u32 arrays shaped `[experts, output, input/8]` and bf16
scales/biases shaped `[experts, output, input/64]`.

The staged execution is effectively:
`slot = expert_to_slot[layer, expert_id]`
`selected_bundle = staged_tensor[layer, slot]`.
Missing slots can be zero, exact-loaded, or dropped depending on mode.

TRAINING REQUIRED:
The streaming runtime itself does not require training. Edge0's Recover-LoRA
adapters require a recovery/fine-tuning procedure. The prerouter is trained
or supplied with checkpoint heads. The public documentation does not give a
complete predictor training dataset/optimization recipe or a full accuracy
breakdown for the predictor alone.

INFERENCE MECHANISM:
Prefill can use a full-layer path: direct stacked tensor load avoids building
256 individual expert bundles. Decode uses exact per-expert, hot-cache, or
staged fixed-slot paths. Hooks prefetch previous-token expert sets and stage
the prior layer's used set. The Qwen engine stages and swaps the next set at
each non-full forward. Hot pinning uses decayed counts plus predicted-expert
bonus; hot data can be materialized for a layer window.

MEMORY BEHAVIOR:
The expert weights remain mmapped/SSD backed and are not all loaded up front.
The global cache has fixed capacity; staging and prefetch buffers are bounded.
Embeddings, attention/linear layers, routers, norms, shared experts, output
head and runtime state remain resident. Peak active memory in README examples
is 2.9 GiB for edge0-35b and 1.0 GiB for edge0-8b, excluding the full mapped
expert address space and adding KV cache for long context.

I/O BEHAVIOR:
Each expert miss causes several byte-range reads. Prefetch submits futures;
the callback inserts completed bundles in the prefetch cache and tracks
wasted prefetch. The system can reuse a global cache across layers. Staged
slots trade exactness for predictable indexing. Public docs/code expose load,
hit, prefetch and wasted-prefetch counters, but the inspected README/docs do
not publish a complete bytes-moved/token or cache-hit table.

THROUGHPUT RESULTS:
README benchmark: Apple M4 Pro, 3.3k prompt tokens, 10 warmup, 200 decode
tokens, two runs. Edge0-35b reports 14.9-17.7 tok/s and 2.9 GiB peak active
memory; edge0-8b reports 23.9-25.3 tok/s and 1.0 GiB. Prerouter is claimed
to improve decode throughput by up to 59%. Prefill cold/warm values are
reported as approximately 113/140 and 500/1428 tok/s for the two examples.
These are Apple/MLX results, not CPU/x86 measurements.

QUALITY RESULTS:
README average score is 79.2 for edge0-35b int4 plus adapters versus 83.2
for its fp16 base, and 69.9 versus 72.7 for edge0-8b. This includes the
sparse/quantized checkpoint and recovery adapters; it does not isolate SSD
miss errors or prerouter errors.

HARDWARE:
Apple M4 Pro, MLX, Apple Silicon. No Linux/x86 path is present in the
inspected repository; CUDA is listed as a future direction.

WHAT ACTUALLY CAUSED THE SPEEDUP:
Resident non-expert computation, 4-bit weights, bounded global reuse,
coalesced expert bundle reads, fixed staged GPU lookup, asynchronous load,
and cross-layer/cross-token prediction. FLOP reduction alone is not enough.

WHAT DID NOT WORK:
The repository supports several fallback paths because staging can miss,
prefetch can be wasted, and exact/hot/full-layer execution are needed for
different phases. Staged execution can represent missing experts as zeros or
must fall back to exact loads. The design is not a general x86 implementation.

LIMITATIONS:
The base model is already sparse MoE, so Edge0 does not answer how to derive
experts from dense Qwen3.8 weights. It does not publish the desired bytes per
token, cache-hit distribution, SSD random-read breakdown, or an 8 GB x86
result. Mmap is a virtual-memory interface, not proof that SSD latency is
hidden. The hybrid model also leaves a resident compute floor.

ASSUMPTIONS:
Expert tensors can be independently ranged from shards; predicted experts are
available early enough; a global cache is better than isolated per-layer
caches; MLX asynchronous execution is safe with the chosen synchronization;
4-bit quantization and LoRA can recover acceptable quality.

TRANSFER TO QWEN3.8-27B:
Copy the expert/neuron bundle layout, global LRU, fixed slots, prefetch queue,
hot pinning, full-layer prefill path, counters and one-layer/one-token
lookahead. Replace native MoE experts with aligned gated-FFN groups and build
a Qwen3.8-specific CPU implementation. Do not assume Edge0's 256-expert
route distribution, quality, Apple latency or bytes/token.

TRANSFER TO LLAMA.CPP:
The conceptual cache/staging/prefetch design transfers. MLX tensors,
`gather_qmm`, Python thread callbacks and Apple execution do not. llama.cpp
will need explicit GGUF tensor records, an x86 sparse/gather kernel and a
storage-aware scheduler that avoids implicit page faults.

WHAT WE SHOULD COPY:
[PRIOR ART] One global cache; separate prefetch buffer; fixed staging slots;
slot-table routing; explicit actual-vs-predicted statistics; full-layer
prefill; hot pinning; early cross-layer/cross-token prediction; exact fallback.

WHAT WE SHOULD NOT COPY:
[PRIOR ART LIMIT] Treating mmap as the entire I/O design; relying on zero/drop
misses without a measured quality guard; assuming Apple/MLX asynchronous
behavior transfers to x86; building one tiny request per neuron; claiming
bytes/token without counters.

OPEN QUESTIONS:
Can Qwen3.8 gated neuron groups achieve enough overlap and reuse at 8 GB?
What fixed group size gives the best CPU/SSD tradeoff? Can a predictor's
false negatives be repaired without synchronous random reads? How should
quantized down columns be repacked?

[S02] MOEFICATION

TITLE: MoEfication: Transformer Feed-forward Layers are Mixtures of Experts
AUTHORS: Zhang et al.
YEAR: 2022
VENUE: Findings of ACL 2022
URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.01786 ; https://aclanthology.org/2022.findings-acl.71/
CODE: https://github.com/thunlp/MoEfication
RELEVANCE: high

PROBLEM:
Exploit the observation that only a small fraction of dense FFN neurons are
strongly active for an input, without initially training an MoE architecture.

KEY IDEA:
Partition an existing FFN's intermediate neurons into experts. Every expert
keeps the corresponding columns of W1/b1 and rows of W2, so selecting a subset
is functionally meaningful. Predict which experts matter for each input and
run only those experts.

ARCHITECTURE:
For a simple ReLU FFN, `h = x W1 + b1`, `F(x)=sigma(h) W2+b2`. Partition
intermediate width `d_ff` into k experts of width `d_ff/k`, after permuting
W1 columns and W2 rows consistently. Selecting all experts is the original
dense FFN; selecting a subset reduces the intermediate work.

ALGORITHM:
The paper constructs experts by neuron coactivation. One stated similarity
is the positive coactivation graph weight
`C(n,m) = sum_x h_n h_m 1(h_n>0 and h_m>0)`, followed by graph partitioning
(METIS). The released fast path also has a FAISS K-means/balanced assignment
implementation. It normalizes W1 neuron vectors, creates expert centers,
and uses center similarity to score groups. A small MLP predicts expert
scores from x; top 20% is selected in the main experiments.

In the code, `MLPCenter` is two linear layers without bias with tanh between;
the first layer is initialized from centers and the second approximately
identity-like. The training script uses BCE against normalized positive
activation-score labels, Adam at lr .01 for 30 epochs. It is a calibration/
predictor training procedure, not dense-model pretraining.

IMPORTANT EQUATIONS:
For expert e with neuron set I_e:
`F_e(x) = sigma(x W1[:,I_e] + b1[I_e]) W2[I_e,:]`.
The full output is the sum over all experts for linear output accumulation.

The similarity predictor cost is reported as approximately `O(k d_model)`;
with expert width 32 this can be `O(d_model^2/8)`, plus the small MLP cost.

TRAINING REQUIRED:
Activation traces/calibration data and router training are required. The
paper relu-fies BERT for its GELU experiment and reports roughly 400 update
steps to adapt versus about 10k pretraining steps, but that does not prove a
safe Qwen3.8 SiLU conversion. It is not training-free.

INFERENCE MECHANISM:
Compute/predict expert scores, choose top-k groups, run their submatrices and
accumulate. Ground-truth selection is an upper bound; a classifier produces
the deployable route.

MEMORY BEHAVIOR:
Total parameter count is approximately unchanged by partitioning. The groups
can be laid out independently, which is the useful bridge to SSD residency.
The paper does not implement SSD paging or a bounded RAM cache.

I/O BEHAVIOR:
Not measured. Grouping creates a potential storage unit, but the paper's
experiments assume weights are already available.

THROUGHPUT RESULTS:
At 50%, 25% and 12.5% selected, reported speed ratios are approximately
1.50/1.43/1.15, 2.00/1.98/1.20 and 2.40/2.28/1.47 for theoretical FLOPs/
CPU/GPU respectively. The CPU/GPU experiments use batch/sequence settings
around 64, Intel Broadwell and Tesla V100; they are not single-token decode.
Attention becomes a bottleneck. The CPU numbers are more relevant than FLOPs
but still do not establish Qwen3.8/x86/SSD behavior.

QUALITY RESULTS:
At 20% selection for T5-Large, original SST2/MNLI/RACE scores are about
96.2/89.5/81.3; coactivation plus MLP is about 95.4/87.5/79.0, while
ground-truth selection is about 96.3/89.1/80.8. Calibration reduces an
average drop reported near 1.5 points to near 0.3 points in the tested cases.
T5-XL examples retain roughly 98% quality at 10% selected for easier tasks;
RACE is harder. Usage has a long tail: general experts can serve over 80%
of examples while input-specific experts serve under 10%.

HARDWARE:
Intel Broadwell CPU and Tesla V100 GPU; older transformer models/T5/BERT.

WHAT ACTUALLY CAUSED THE SPEEDUP:
Aligned neuron partitioning plus structured submatrix computation; not disk
offload. CPU wall time follows arithmetic better than GPU, but attention and
router overhead limit end-to-end gains.

WHAT DID NOT WORK:
Naive/ground-truth interpretation is not a deployable router; classifier
errors reduce quality. ReLU assumptions and expert granularity are important.
The method does not solve small SSD reads, gated activations or continuous
nonzero SiLU outputs.

LIMITATIONS:
The most important limitation for Qwen is the activation. A simple ReLU
positive/nonpositive mask does not exist for `silu(gate) * up`; up values can
be negative and SiLU is not exactly zero on negative inputs. Old experiment
percentages cannot be transferred. The reported wall-clock experiments use
larger batches/sequence processing than our single-token target.

ASSUMPTIONS:
Neurons are separable into groups; selected groups preserve enough function;
the router can be evaluated cheaply; submatrices can be computed efficiently.

TRANSFER TO QWEN3.8-27B:
Partition the intermediate index jointly across gate, up and down. For group
I, load gate rows I, up rows I and down columns I. Use contribution-aware
scores, not just positive gate counts:
`q_i(x)=|silu(gate_i(x))*up_i(x)|^2 * ||down[:,i]||^2`.
Group scores should include within-group interference/correlation. A first
calibration can use exact full FFN outputs and measure grouped truncation.

TRANSFER TO LLAMA.CPP:
The grouping/reordering idea transfers. A GGUF converter must repack aligned
groups and a kernel must gather contiguous group rows/columns. Generic dense
GGML matmul does not automatically obtain the benefit.

WHAT WE SHOULD COPY:
[PRIOR ART] Function-preserving neuron permutation, aligned W1/W2 grouping,
calibration labels, a small score predictor, and comparison against
ground-truth group selection.

WHAT WE SHOULD NOT COPY:
[PRIOR ART LIMIT] ReLU positivity labels, “10% active” claims for Qwen,
unstructured tiny groups, or CPU speed ratios measured at batch 64.

OPEN QUESTIONS:
What group size balances contribution error with SSD sequentiality? Does
coactivation computed on gated contributions remain stable over long decode?
Can a group predictor be trained from only a small calibration set without
false negatives that force synchronous reads?

[S03] LLM IN A FLASH

TITLE: LLM in a flash: Efficient Large Language Model Inference with Limited Memory
AUTHORS: Alizadeh et al.
YEAR: 2024
VENUE: ACL 2024
URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.11514 ; https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-long.678/
CODE: No primary runtime repository identified in the paper record.
RELEVANCE: high

PROBLEM:
Run LLMs when model weights exceed DRAM by using flash storage without turning
every token into a catastrophic random-I/O workload.

KEY IDEA:
Persist embeddings and attention in DRAM, predict only the useful FFN
neurons, read them in windows, reuse recent neurons, and bundle corresponding
up/down data so flash transfers are larger and more sequential.

ARCHITECTURE:
The selective-persistence design keeps approximately one third of the model
(embedding plus attention) resident and treats active FFN rows/columns as a
working set. The paper uses ReLU/FATReLU-like sparsity and low-rank predictors.

ALGORITHM:
A predictor uses an attention output to estimate positive up-projection
neurons before loading them. The predictor is trained on 10k C4 samples for
two epochs and deliberately overpredicts by about 3x. Reported OPT false
negative and false positive rates are approximately 5% and 7% in one setup,
with later layers more sensitive. A low-rank predictor is used to reduce
overhead.

Windowing with k=4 or 5 reuses recently active sets. The incremental read is
conceptually `S_agg(k+1) - S_agg(k)` instead of reloading the entire current
set. Row-column bundling stores the ith up column with the ith down row. The
paper notes that naive hot-neuron bundling can be negative when a row/column
has high degree, so coactivation and layout must be measured.

IMPORTANT EQUATIONS / COST MODEL:
For a token, a useful first-order latency decomposition is:
`T_token = T_I/O + T_memory_management + T_compute`.

Flash has higher first-byte latency for small/random access, but large reads
(reported around 32 KiB or more) can be serviced with many threads. On an M1
Max, the paper reports approximately 6.1 GiB/s for dense sequential reads and
1.25 GiB/s for sparse/random access in a relevant configuration.

The target storage traffic is the selected weight volume. For this project:
`B_effective_per_token = bandwidth / tokens_per_second`.
Thus 4 GB/s / 10 tok/s = 400 MB/token and 4 GB/s / 20 tok/s = 200 MB/token
as absolute upper bounds; filesystem, queue, CPU and overlap reduce them.

TRAINING REQUIRED:
Predictor training and calibration. The base model is not converted to MoE.

INFERENCE MECHANISM:
Predict, issue grouped reads, allocate fixed DRAM rows, copy active up/down
pieces, and reuse a sliding window. The implementation uses many read threads
and bypasses OS cache in throughput measurements to make I/O explicit.

MEMORY BEHAVIOR:
Approximately half of the model is placed outside DRAM in the headline
experiments; embeddings/attention remain resident. Preallocated rows avoid
fragmentation and swap overhead. This is a bounded working-set strategy, not
a proof that an arbitrary 27B model fits below 8 GB.

I/O BEHAVIOR:
Naive random sparse reads are slow. A reported OPT 6.7B, 16-bit example gives
approximately 6.10 GB/s and 2196 ms for naive I/O, 1.09 GB/s and 738 ms with
the predictor, then about 0.2 GB and 164 ms with windowing, and about 0.2 GB,
2.25 GB/s and 87 ms with bundling in one comparison table. These table values
are component measurements, not a universal per-token law.

THROUGHPUT RESULTS:
Single sequence, approximately 128 prompt + 256 generation tokens. OPT 6.7B
end-to-end times are reported as about 669 ms for the proposed CPU path versus
3182 ms for the naive baseline on M1; M1/M2/GPU variants are also reported.
Falcon, Persimmon and Phi2 examples are approximately 706, 1041 and 546 ms
in the table. Hardware is Apple M1/M2 and GPU variants, not x86 NVMe.

QUALITY RESULTS:
For Llama2 in the sparse setup, MMLU is reported around 41.8 dense,
38.96 sparse and 38.63 predictor. This is a real quality cost, not merely a
FLOP result. The paper also evaluates perplexity and model-specific tasks.

HARDWARE:
Apple M1 Max/M1/M2 and associated GPU/Metal/PyTorch/MLX-style systems. Single
sequence; not llama.cpp and not commodity Linux CPU.

WHAT ACTUALLY CAUSED THE SPEEDUP:
Selective persistence, overprediction to reduce false negatives, temporal
window reuse, large row/column transfers and preallocated buffers. The key
lesson is layout plus reuse, not mmap by itself.

WHAT DID NOT WORK:
Naive paging/random reads; small read granularity; predictor false negatives;
bundling without coactivation analysis. Memory management is a first-class
term in the cost model.

LIMITATIONS:
ReLU/FATReLU assumptions, Apple storage/memory stack, no Qwen3.8 gated FFN,
no x86 CPU results, and roughly half-model DRAM rather than an 8 GB absolute
cap. The reported speedups do not directly imply 10-20 tok/s for a 27B model.

ASSUMPTIONS:
Active neurons can be predicted from current context; recent active sets are
reusable; flash large reads are much more efficient than tiny reads; attention
and embeddings fit in resident memory.

TRANSFER TO QWEN3.8-27B:
Copy the cost model, resident-vs-streamed partition, sliding-window reuse,
coactivation-based bundling and fixed rows. Replace positive ReLU neuron
selection with contribution-aware gated-group selection and measure whether
Qwen token-to-token overlap is high enough.

TRANSFER TO LLAMA.CPP:
Copy the explicit storage scheduler and packed layout. Avoid relying on
generic page faults. A C++ implementation should expose reads/token,
read-size histogram, queue depth, cache hits, overlap and stall time.

WHAT WE SHOULD COPY:
[PRIOR ART] Window reuse; up/down row-column bundles; resident attention
floor; overprediction policy; preallocated staging; bandwidth-first accounting.

WHAT WE SHOULD NOT COPY:
[PRIOR ART LIMIT] ReLU sparsity rates or Apple timings as Qwen/x86 estimates;
uncounted random I/O; and a predictor trained only on prompt contexts if its
distribution shifts during generation.

OPEN QUESTIONS:
What is the equivalent of FATReLU for unmodified Qwen SwiGLU? Is a group
large enough for SSD reads still accurate? Does Qwen's hybrid linear attention
produce stronger or weaker active-set reuse than OPT?

[S04] POWERINFER-2

TITLE: PowerInfer-2: Fast Large Language Model Inference on a Smartphone
AUTHORS: PowerInfer-2 authors
YEAR: 2024
VENUE: arXiv preprint
URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.06282 ; https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.06282
CODE: https://github.com/SJTU-IPADS/PowerInfer ; https://powerinfer.ai/v2/
RELEVANCE: high

PROBLEM:
Run models larger than smartphone memory by making neuron activation and
storage/compute scheduling aware of heterogeneous NPU, CPU and UFS behavior.

KEY IDEA:
Use neuron clusters as the unit of activation/I/O, keep frequently used hot
clusters in a segmented cache, stream cold clusters through a pipelined gate/
up/down execution path, and place work on NPU or CPU according to locality.

ARCHITECTURE:
For a typical 14336 x 4096 FFN, a 50% offload example uses 7168 hot neurons
and 7168 cold neurons. CPU cold chunks are around 512 x 4096. Cluster sizes
adapt to activation similarity. Decode uses NPU dense hot clusters and CPU
sparse cold work; prefill can use dense NPU processing.

ALGORITHM:
Clusters with similar activation are created offline. Gate/up/down for a
neuron are bundled. After removing hot neurons, the paper reports about 80%
coactivation for corresponding Gate+Up+Down. It can read the gate first and
skip up/down for a zero gate, saving around 20% wasted work in its design.

The segmented cache has fixed attention/KV state, a hot region of dense
clusters with LRU replacement, and a cold region with neuron-level replacement.
The execution pipeline has gate I/O/calculation followed by up/down I/O/
calculation, using one I/O thread plus compute threads to break matrix barriers.

IMPORTANT EQUATIONS:
The paper's core scheduling cost is operational rather than a single new
equation: active cluster data is selected by a device-specific hot/cold
profile; corresponding gate/up/down records are transferred as a unit.

TRAINING REQUIRED:
Offline profiling on 10M+ Wikipedia/RefinedWeb tokens and device-specific
hot/cold classification/centroids. Predictor/placement preparation is not
free. The base model is not converted into a MoE.

INFERENCE MECHANISM:
Hot clusters run in the NPU-resident path; cold clusters are prefetched and
processed by CPU threads; segmented caches and pipeline overlap storage with
compute. Device-specific placement is central.

MEMORY BEHAVIOR:
One reported Mixtral47B setup at a 7 GB memory cap allocates approximately
1 GB non-FFN, 2.6 GB predictor, 2.7 GB scales and 0.4 GB runtime/cache in the
breakdown, leaving only about 1.8% of FFN weights cached. This illustrates how
metadata/predictor/scales can consume the memory budget.

I/O BEHAVIOR:
The paper reports UFS sequential around 4 GB/s versus PC SSD around 7.5 GB/s,
but random IOPS differ greatly. UFS 4 KB random behavior and a single command
queue make thread count important; multiple I/O cores can degrade throughput
by up to about 40%. The design uses cluster-sized transfers and one I/O thread
in the critical pipeline.

THROUGHPUT RESULTS:
On OnePlus12/Ace2, Mistral7B with 50% FFN offload is about 1.4 tok/s for
PowerInfer-2 versus 2.3 tok/s for LLM in a Flash in one comparison. A
Mixtral47B result is 11.68 tok/s at 19 GB and 2.13 tok/s at 7 GB, with a
reported 21.2x improvement over llama.cpp in its setup. The 7 GB point is the
more relevant warning: aggressive memory caps leave I/O dominant.

QUALITY RESULTS:
PowerInfer-2 reports quality comparable to llama.cpp in its evaluated models;
one Qwen2 average is about 78.38 versus 79.25 for llama.cpp. Exact task and
model details must be preserved when comparing.

HARDWARE:
OnePlus12/Ace2 smartphone, Arm CPU, NPU, UFS storage, unified/mobile memory.

WHAT ACTUALLY CAUSED THE SPEEDUP:
Cluster-level coalescing, segmented cache, gate-first filtering, explicit
I/O/compute pipeline, and device-specific hot placement. Not portable NPU
throughput alone.

WHAT DID NOT WORK:
At low memory the cache is tiny and storage remains dominant. UFS random
reads and excessive I/O threads degrade. A generic CPU/SSD scheduler cannot
assume the same cache or queue behavior.

LIMITATIONS:
Mobile NPU/CPU and Android implementation; no Linux/x86 CPU-only result; the
predictor and scales consume a large fraction of a small cap; native sparse
models/modified model variants are used.

ASSUMPTIONS:
Activation clusters are stable; a hot subset is globally reusable; NPU can
run dense hot work; cluster reads amortize storage latency.

TRANSFER TO QWEN3.8-27B:
Copy neuron/group bundling, segmented hot/cold cache, gate-first filtering,
one-I/O-thread pipeline, and explicit small-cap memory accounting. Test
whether Qwen SwiGLU gate values can safely filter up/down groups; do not
assume mobile NPU placement or cluster quality.

TRANSFER TO LLAMA.CPP:
The segment/cache/pipeline concepts transfer; NPU scheduling and Android
memory mechanisms do not. A CPU implementation should start with fixed group
records and sequential ranges, then benchmark whether a sparse CPU kernel is
worth the bookkeeping.

WHAT WE SHOULD COPY:
[PRIOR ART] Cluster I/O unit; corresponding gate/up/down storage; segmented
cache; gate-first two-stage pipeline; device-specific profiling.

WHAT WE SHOULD NOT COPY:
[PRIOR ART LIMIT] Mobile-specific hot/cold ratios, NPU/CPU split, and a memory
budget that hides predictor/scales costs in a different way.

OPEN QUESTIONS:
Can Qwen group records be made 8-32 KiB each without too much overfetch? Is a
CPU gate-first phase cheaper than reading both gate and up together? How much
predictor state can be kept below 8 GB?

[S05] SPARSE UPCYCLING

TITLE: Sparse Upcycling: Training Mixture-of-Experts from Dense Checkpoints
AUTHORS: Komatsuzaki et al.
YEAR: 2023
VENUE: ICLR 2023
URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.05055 ; https://arxiv.org/html/2212.05055v2
CODE: Paper artifacts/checkpoint details are linked from the paper; no
      storage runtime is supplied.
RELEVANCE: high

PROBLEM:
Obtain a sparse MoE model without paying the full cost of training a large
MoE from random initialization.

KEY IDEA:
Copy a dense checkpoint into multiple identical experts, add a router, and
continue training. The copied initialization starts near the dense function;
continued training breaks symmetry and specializes experts.

ARCHITECTURE:
Experiments use T5 encoder/decoder models, with MoE layers inserted in a
subset of layers (for example six layers) and up to 32 experts. The decoder
uses top-k routing (often k=2); expert choice routing is used in some encoder
settings. The copied experts initially compute a redundant version of the
dense FFN.

ALGORITHM:
Dense weights are copied into each expert. Router weights are newly
initialized. Expert capacity and token routing follow standard sparse MoE
mechanics. The important initialization is copied weights, not random expert
weights. Identical copies create a symmetry problem: only continued training
with different routes/gradients can specialize them.

IMPORTANT EQUATIONS:
MoE output is the standard routed sum:
`y = sum_{e in TopK(router(x))} p_e(x) E_e(x)`.
At initialization, copied experts have `E_e ~= E_dense`, so selecting all or
appropriately weighted copies preserves the dense function, but top-k routing
and normalization can still change scale/output.

TRAINING REQUIRED:
Substantial continued training, reported in the range of roughly 10-60%
additional training budget depending on the experiment. The paper reports
that upcycling catches a sparse-from-scratch baseline at around 120% of the
dense training budget in relevant comparisons. More experts and more severe
changes increase the requirement.

INFERENCE MECHANISM:
A conventional router chooses experts. Active compute is generally kept near
the dense baseline while total capacity increases; this is the key mismatch
with our objective.

MEMORY BEHAVIOR:
Total parameters rise sharply. Illustrative T5 sizes are approximately
248M -> 2B, 783M -> 7.22B and 2.85B -> 26.26B for sparse variants. Expert
weights could be stored on SSD, but the paper does not do so.

I/O BEHAVIOR:
Not studied.

THROUGHPUT RESULTS:
No storage or single-token CPU wall-clock result. Sparse MoE FLOPs are lower
than a dense model of the same expanded capacity, not necessarily lower than
the original dense checkpoint.

QUALITY RESULTS:
Copied initialization is better than random under limited continued training;
random can catch up with more training. The results establish a practical
dense-to-MoE route, not a no-training or lower-active-FLOP conversion.

HARDWARE:
Large-scale TPU training, T5 models.

WHAT ACTUALLY CAUSED THE SPEEDUP:
The technique's value is training cost/quality and capacity scaling. It does
not demonstrate lower active compute than the source dense model.

WHAT DID NOT WORK:
Naively copying many experts without enough training leaves symmetry and
specialization problems. Applying sparse-upcycling as if it automatically
reduced source-model compute is incorrect.

LIMITATIONS:
It increases total parameters and commonly preserves comparable active FLOPs.
It requires continued training and routing changes. It is not a storage
layout or predictor solution.

ASSUMPTIONS:
Training data and compute are available to break symmetry; standard MoE
routing and capacity mechanisms are acceptable.

TRANSFER TO QWEN3.8-27B:
Borrow copied initialization and symmetry-breaking logic only if we decide to
train a genuine Qwen sparse expert model. To reduce active compute below
dense, expert width and/or top-k must also be reduced; that creates a quality
problem the original paper does not answer.

TRANSFER TO LLAMA.CPP:
Only after a trained sparse checkpoint exists: expert serialization/routing
could be supported. Upcycling itself gives no llama.cpp storage solution.

WHAT WE SHOULD COPY:
[PRIOR ART] Dense checkpoint copying as a stable initialization and explicit
measurement of additional training budget/symmetry.

WHAT WE SHOULD NOT COPY:
[PRIOR ART LIMIT] Treat “sparse MoE” as “lower active compute than dense”; do
not multiply experts and then assume an 8 GB model.

OPEN QUESTIONS:
How much can copied group experts be narrowed before fine-tuning becomes
prohibitive? Could distillation from the dense Qwen3.8 model replace most of
the continued pretraining?

[S06] NVIDIA DENSE-TO-MOE UPCYCLING

TITLE: Upcycling Large Language Models into Mixture of Experts
AUTHORS: He et al. / NVIDIA authors
YEAR: 2024
VENUE: arXiv preprint
URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.07524 ; https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2410.07524
CODE: No storage runtime in the paper record.
RELEVANCE: high

PROBLEM:
Convert billion-scale dense LLMs into fine-grained MoE models while retaining
quality and making training/inference tradeoffs explicit.

KEY IDEA:
Partition dense FFN weights into virtual groups, replicate them into many
fine-grained experts, scale the copied weights, and continue training. Virtual
groups preserve a structured path back to the dense matrix; routing chooses a
small number of groups.

ARCHITECTURE:
For dense groups G and replicated experts E, experts contain shards of the
dense FFN. Typical experiments compare E=8, group counts such as G=1 or 8,
and top-T routing such as T=2 or 8. The method includes experiments on
Nemotron-scale 2B/15B models and gated FFNs.

ALGORITHM:
Basic copy partitions and replicates weights. With topK-then-softmax,
normalization after selection can preserve the dense function only in a
particular all-replica limit. Softmax-then-topK retains absolute router
logit information and is empirically better in the paper. For squared ReLU,
the paper gives a scaling related to `T/(E G^2)` and rescales W1/W2 using a
cube-root factor; equal scaling is reported to work for SwiGLU in practice.
Auxiliary load balancing is around 1e-2 to 1e-3; too high harms quality.

IMPORTANT EQUATIONS:
The relevant initialization has a group/expert scale of the form
`scale ~ (E G^2 / T)^(1/3)` for the paired projections in one stated
derivation, with the inverse convention depending on which tensor is being
normalized. The exact paper equation must be followed in code; the key lesson
is that replicated fine-grained shards need explicit magnitude correction.

TRAINING REQUIRED:
Continued pretraining. One reported comparison uses one trillion examples/
tokens of dense continued training and then compares E8G8T8 or E8G1T2 MoE
variants. More experts have diminishing returns; 64 is generally useful while
128/256 add less.

INFERENCE MECHANISM:
Router logits -> chosen fine-grained experts -> gated FFN. T=2 costs more
than top-1; the paper studies quality/computation rather than SSD paging.

MEMORY BEHAVIOR:
Total capacity rises with replication. Fine-grained experts can make storage
units smaller, but metadata and random reads become harder. No RAM/SSD result.

I/O BEHAVIOR:
Not reported.

THROUGHPUT RESULTS:
Quality/computation tradeoffs and iso-FLOP comparisons are reported, not
single-stream CPU wall time or storage bytes/token.

QUALITY RESULTS:
Examples include dense continued val loss 1.377 / MMLU 65.3 versus E8G8T8
val loss 1.320 / MMLU 66.2, and E8G1T2 val loss 1.306 / MMLU 67.6 in the
reported comparisons. High load-balance loss harms quality; group granularity
around 8 is favorable; a shared expert did not clearly help.

HARDWARE:
NVIDIA-scale training, billion-scale models.

WHAT ACTUALLY CAUSED THE SPEEDUP:
Conditional active experts at a fixed expanded capacity, plus fine-grained
routing. It does not show lower active compute than the original dense model
without a separate width/top-k choice.

WHAT DID NOT WORK:
Excessive expert granularity/routing overhead, too-high auxiliary loss,
naive scaling, and assuming more experts always improves quality.

LIMITATIONS:
Requires substantial continued training and does not solve storage. The
reported models are not Qwen3.8 and no x86/8 GB target is evaluated.

ASSUMPTIONS:
Large continued-training budget; standard MoE router; expert replication is
acceptable.

TRANSFER TO QWEN3.8-27B:
Borrow virtual-group initialization, explicit projection scaling and the
softmax-order comparison if a trained MoE branch is attempted. Gated SwiGLU
compatibility is encouraging. Do not assume the method gives a post-hoc
low-compute checkpoint; it is a training recipe.

TRANSFER TO LLAMA.CPP:
Expert granularity can inform on-disk group size, but routing/checkpoint
conversion must be implemented separately.

WHAT WE SHOULD COPY:
[PRIOR ART] Virtual groups, projection scaling, load-balance loss sweeps,
granularity ablations, and explicit active-compute accounting.

WHAT WE SHOULD NOT COPY:
[PRIOR ART LIMIT] Continued-training results as evidence for training-free
conversion or SSD throughput.

OPEN QUESTIONS:
Can a tiny distillation run reproduce useful virtual-group specialization?
Does fine-grained replication make SSD reads too random for a CPU decoder?

[S07] DEJAVU

TITLE: Deja Vu: Contextual Sparsity for Efficient LLMs at Inference Time
AUTHORS: Liu et al.
YEAR: 2023
VENUE: ICML 2023 / arXiv 2023
URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.17157 ; https://proceedings.mlr.press/v202/liu23am/liu23am.pdf
CODE: https://github.com/FMInference/DejaVu, commit ee902c1
RELEVANCE: high

PROBLEM:
Use input-dependent sparsity in a dense pretrained LLM without requiring a
MoE architecture up front.

KEY IDEA:
Predict which attention heads and MLP parameters will matter for the current
context, then run structured sparse kernels. Prediction is asynchronous and
lookahead exploits the slow change of consecutive hidden states.

ARCHITECTURE:
Contextual sparsity selects attention heads and MLP intermediate rows/columns.
The sparse MLP is `MLP_S(y)=sigma(y W1_S) W2_S^T`. For OPT-175B the paper
reports up to about 80% head sparsity and 95% MLP parameter sparsity in a
tested regime.

ALGORITHM:
The predictor is a two-layer fully connected classifier, not online nearest
neighbor search. HNSW/FAISS were too slow in the cited comparison (over 10 ms
and over 4 ms), while a small MLP was about 0.2 ms for OPT-175B. Code uses a
low-rank/parallel classifier: embedding -> low-rank dimension (example D=1000)
-> output logits. Training uses memmapped predictor data, BCE with weighted
positives, AdamW, default 20 epochs, lr 1e-4 and roughly 400k samples in the
repository training script. The paper describes a 500 C4 sample setup for
some predictor construction; exact data scale differs by experiment.

The async predictor estimates the next layer's set from the current output.
An informal slow-change argument is that if `||y_l-y_{l-1}|| <= epsilon`, a
MaxIP-style set remains close enough for high recall. The real implementation
runs the predictor on a separate CUDA stream and synchronizes with an event.

Attention prediction can save selected KV heads and token embeddings so a
missing head can be recomputed later. Sparse MLP kernels gather W1 rows and
W2 columns; the repository uses a layout chosen for coalesced indexed loads.

IMPORTANT EQUATIONS:
`S_A^l = SP_A^l(y_l)` and `S_M^l = SP_M^l(MHA(y_l))` are the native current-
layer predictor sets. The asynchronous lookahead uses current activations to
predict `S_A^(l+1), S_M^(l+1)` while current work proceeds.

TRAINING REQUIRED:
Small predictor training/calibration on activation/mask labels. The base LLM
weights remain unchanged.

INFERENCE MECHANISM:
Predict mask, gather selected rows/columns, run fused sparse kernels. Predictors
are launched early and hidden behind current MLP/attention work where the GPU
allows it. This hides predictor cost but does not automatically hide SSD reads.

MEMORY BEHAVIOR:
Sparse kernels avoid loading all selected-unselected rows into GPU memory.
The paper's primary setting is large A100 memory; it does not maintain an SSD
resident cold pool.

I/O BEHAVIOR:
The paper is about GPU memory traffic, not flash. Its fused indexing avoids a
separate indexing pass that would multiply weight traffic. No SSD bytes/token.

THROUGHPUT RESULTS:
Batch 1 on 8 A100 80 GB GPUs with NVLink, FP16, prompt lengths 128-1024.
At a reported regime of roughly 75% sparsity, DejaVu is 1.8-2x faster than
FasterTransformer and 4.8-6x faster than Hugging Face in the paper's setup.
These are actual wall-clock results but not CPU/SSD decode.

QUALITY RESULTS:
OPT-175B/66B/30B tests report no accuracy drop at selected regimes in the
paper tables, with MLP around 85% sparse and attention around 50% sparse for
one OPT-175B setting. Predictor recall varies by depth; the paper reports
shallow MLP over 99% improving to roughly 93%, and attention middle layers
around 93% with shallow/deep around 99% in one analysis. W4A16 compatibility
is discussed and tested without compounding errors in the table.

HARDWARE:
8x NVIDIA A100 80 GB NVLink; CUDA/Triton custom kernels.

WHAT ACTUALLY CAUSED THE SPEEDUP:
Structured indexing kernels, high mask recall, predictor overlap, reduced
GPU memory traffic and attention/MLP sparsity. Not merely sparse FLOPs.

WHAT DID NOT WORK:
Nearest-neighbor search predictors were too slow; predictors run too late if
not overlapped; low recall causes quality errors; GPU kernels are necessary.

LIMITATIONS:
The models/activations are mostly older dense ReLU/GELU-era LLMs. It does not
show storage reads, x86 CPU kernels, Qwen3.8 hybrid blocks or <8 GB RAM. A
per-layer predictor can be too late to fetch from SSD.

ASSUMPTIONS:
Temporal/context locality; predictor overhead can overlap current GPU work;
indexed sparse matrix kernels are efficient; mask errors are rare enough.

TRANSFER TO QWEN3.8-27B:
Use contextual sparsity as a router without converting all MLPs to MoE. Train
or construct an early shared predictor and measure gated contribution masks.
Borrow lookahead, but move the prediction horizon earlier than DejaVu's
within-layer operation so storage reads can complete.

TRANSFER TO LLAMA.CPP:
Architecture/predictor idea transfers. CUDA streams/Triton kernels do not.
On CPU, predictor cost and gather overhead must be measured alongside I/O.

WHAT WE SHOULD COPY:
[PRIOR ART] Shallow predictors, async lookahead, structured W1/W2 indexing,
recall-first evaluation, and predictor overhead accounting.

WHAT WE SHOULD NOT COPY:
[PRIOR ART LIMIT] Per-layer-late prediction for SSD, GPU speedups as CPU
results, or head sparsity without a Qwen hybrid-quality test.

OPEN QUESTIONS:
Can one early predictor predict all 64 Qwen MLP layers accurately enough?
Does a group-level mask improve storage locality without losing too much
recall? Can predictor execution overlap CPU attention rather than GPU work?

[S08] POWERINFER

TITLE: PowerInfer: Fast Large Language Model Serving with a Consumer-grade GPU
AUTHORS: Song et al.
YEAR: 2023
VENUE: MLSys/ arXiv 2023
URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.12456 ; https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.12456
CODE: https://github.com/SJTU-IPADS/PowerInfer
RELEVANCE: high

PROBLEM:
Serve models larger than consumer GPU memory by exploiting nonuniform neuron
activation and a CPU/GPU split.

KEY IDEA:
Activation frequencies follow a power-law. Place globally hot neurons on GPU
and context-dependent cold neurons on CPU; use an online predictor and sparse
neuron-aware operators.

ARCHITECTURE:
The method keeps hot neurons resident on the GPU and computes cold neurons on
the CPU, then merges results. It does not convert the model to MoE and does
not use SSD as the cold pool.

ALGORITHM:
An offline profiler measures activation frequencies on about one million
Wikipedia tokens, while an online predictor handles contextual variation.
Placement is formulated using activation frequency/impact and an ILP-like
policy. Sparse operators handle selected neuron rows/columns. Predictor sizes
are reported around 4.88-8.68% of model parameters and training can take
hours on multiple GPUs.

IMPORTANT EQUATIONS:
The useful empirical rule is the activation frequency/impact priority
`v_i ~= f_i` for hot placement. It is a placement heuristic, not a general
quality guarantee. Sparse FFN work is grouped by active neuron indices and
merged with hot GPU output.

TRAINING REQUIRED:
Offline hot/cold profiling and predictor training. No dense-to-MoE training.

INFERENCE MECHANISM:
GPU hot dense path + CPU cold sparse path + synchronization/merge. Batch 1 is
the target in the main consumer-GPU comparison; speedup falls with larger
batches.

MEMORY BEHAVIOR:
Hot neurons consume GPU memory; cold weights remain CPU-resident. This proves
the “resident hot core + external cold pool” abstraction but not SSD residency.

I/O BEHAVIOR:
PCIe transfers rather than SSD. False predictor choices and synchronization
are important; the paper reports examples where a hot neuron can be predicted
inactive.

THROUGHPUT RESULTS:
PC-High uses i9-13900K, 8 cores, 192 GB RAM and RTX4090 24 GB; PC-Low uses
i7-12700K, 8 cores, 64 GB RAM and RTX2080Ti 11 GB. INT4 batch-one averages
are about 13.2 tok/s on the high-end setup, with peak 29.08 and average
speedup around 2.89 (maximum about 4.28); OPT-175B is close to 2 tok/s and
about 2.66x in the reported setting. These are not storage measurements.

QUALITY RESULTS:
The paper reports good quality preservation across supported models while
using hot/cold placement. LLaMA2/ReGLU/SwiGLU activation distributions are
measured, not assumed.

HARDWARE:
Consumer NVIDIA GPUs plus x86 CPUs and PCIe, not CPU-only SSD.

WHAT ACTUALLY CAUSED THE SPEEDUP:
Hot global reuse, sparse cold operators, predictor placement and avoiding
GPU-memory capacity failure. It explicitly exploits the activation distribution.

WHAT DID NOT WORK:
Predictor mismatch and synchronization can erase gains. The CPU/GPU split is
not a flash scheduler.

LIMITATIONS:
The reported hot percentages differ by activation: ReLU OPT30B about 26%
neurons account for 80% activations, LLaMA2 ReGLU70B about 43%, and SwiGLU
LLaMA270B about 69% in one table; across-model percentages also differ. These
are global frequency observations, not per-token safe sparsity. Predictor
training and PCIe/GPU assumptions do not transfer unchanged.

ASSUMPTIONS:
Global hot neurons are stable across tasks; cold work can be sparse on CPU;
GPU hot capacity and PCIe transfers are available.

TRANSFER TO QWEN3.8-27B:
Strongly supports a resident hot core plus SSD cold pool, but global hot
groups should be used as cache candidates, not as the entire route. Profile
Qwen3.8 gated groups and retain high-frequency groups in RAM.

TRANSFER TO LLAMA.CPP:
CPU sparse operators, frequency-based cache selection and predictor error
accounting transfer. GPU/PCIe placement does not.

WHAT WE SHOULD COPY:
[PRIOR ART] Power-law/hot-core measurement, offline profile plus online
prediction, impact-aware placement, and explicit merge/synchronization costs.

WHAT WE SHOULD NOT COPY:
[PRIOR ART LIMIT] Hot percentages as guaranteed per-token activity or GPU
speedup as SSD performance.

OPEN QUESTIONS:
How much of Qwen3.8's active set is globally hot? Does hot-core residency
lower SSD reads enough for 10-20 tok/s? How should cache placement account for
the different 48 linear and 16 full-attention layers?

[S09] SPARSEGPT

TITLE: SparseGPT: Massive Language Models Can be Accurately Pruned in One-Shot
AUTHORS: Frantar and Alistarh
YEAR: 2023
VENUE: ICML 2023
URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.00774 ; https://proceedings.mlr.press/v202/frantar23a/frantar23a.pdf
CODE: https://github.com/IST-DASLab/sparsegpt
RELEVANCE: medium-high

PROBLEM:
Remove static weight redundancy from very large LMs using a single calibration
pass rather than retraining.

KEY IDEA:
Use a Hessian/OBS-inspired one-shot update to choose a sparse mask and
reconstruct remaining weights. It supports unstructured sparsity and some
structured N:M patterns.

ARCHITECTURE:
Layerwise pruning treats each linear weight matrix independently. It is not
contextual and does not create experts.

ALGORITHM:
The layer objective is approximately
`min_{M,W} ||W X - (M * W_hat) X||_2^2`.
For a fixed mask, reconstruction uses a Hessian inverse. A stated OBS-style
deletion update is
`delta_m = -w_m / [H^-1]_{mm} * H^-1_{:,m}`
with estimated deletion error
`epsilon_m = w_m^2 / [H^-1]_{mm}`.
Calibration uses a small multiple of the layer input dimension and block
processing (often block 128); the core complexity is cubic in hidden dimension
for the Hessian operations.

TRAINING REQUIRED:
No gradient training, but calibration activations are required.

INFERENCE MECHANISM:
Static sparse weights are used for every token. There is no router/predictor.

MEMORY BEHAVIOR:
Can reduce stored weight size and potentially resident RAM. Unstructured
sparsity may need specialized kernels; 2:4/4:8 is more hardware-friendly.

I/O BEHAVIOR:
Static reduction lowers every future read but does not reduce context-dependent
working-set volume. A structured packed format is necessary for SSD efficiency.

THROUGHPUT RESULTS:
The paper emphasizes perplexity and sparsity; exact single-stream x86 SSD
throughput is not a result.

QUALITY RESULTS:
OPT/BLOOM around 50-60% unstructured sparsity is reported with small
perplexity changes. Example OPT-175B PPL is about 8.21 at 50% versus dense
8.35; 4:8 about 8.45 and 2:4 about 8.74 in one table.

HARDWARE:
Large-model calibration and sparse hardware experiments; not our storage
runtime.

WHAT ACTUALLY CAUSED THE SPEEDUP:
Permanent parameter removal/structured hardware sparsity, not contextual
selection.

WHAT DID NOT WORK:
Unstructured masks can fail to translate to wall-clock CPU speedups; high
structured sparsity costs quality; it cannot distinguish “inactive now” from
“needed later.”

LIMITATIONS:
It is static pruning, not a solution to conditional SSD residency. It may
also interact badly with Qwen gated rows if the three aligned projections are
pruned independently.

ASSUMPTIONS:
Calibration distribution represents deployment; remaining weights can be
computed efficiently.

TRANSFER TO QWEN3.8-27B:
Use as an optional permanent first stage to reduce all group records and the
resident floor. Apply jointly or consistently to gate/up/down structure. Do
not treat it as a replacement for contextual routing.

TRANSFER TO LLAMA.CPP:
N:M or block-structured static packing is more plausible than arbitrary masks.
It could reduce the amount of every SSD group, but kernel/layout work remains.

WHAT WE SHOULD COPY:
[PRIOR ART] Calibration-aware reconstruction and the distinction between
weight redundancy and contextual inactivity.

WHAT WE SHOULD NOT COPY:
[PRIOR ART LIMIT] Unstructured sparsity as a guaranteed CPU/SSD wall-clock
gain or independent pruning of gated projections.

OPEN QUESTIONS:
Can static group-aware pruning reduce Qwen's 9.6 GB MLP pool enough to make a
dynamic SSD design feasible without consuming the quality budget first?

[S10] SWITCH TRANSFORMERS

TITLE: Switch Transformers: Scaling to Trillion Parameter Models with Simple and Efficient Sparsity
AUTHORS: Fedus, Zoph and Shazeer
YEAR: 2021/2022
VENUE: JMLR 2022
URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.03961 ; https://jmlr.org/papers/v23/21-0998.html
CODE: No storage implementation used.
RELEVANCE: high for MoE mechanics

PROBLEM:
Scale parameter capacity with sparse expert routing while keeping per-token
compute and communication manageable.

KEY IDEA:
Top-1 routing: each token is sent to one expert, minimizing router and
communication cost relative to top-2 MoE.

ARCHITECTURE:
Router logits `h = W_r x`, probabilities `p_i=softmax(h)_i`; selected expert
output is `E_argmax(p)(x)` with a gating scale. Capacity is bounded per expert.

ALGORITHM:
Expert capacity is approximately
`capacity = tokens_per_batch / num_experts * capacity_factor`.
Overflow tokens can be dropped and passed through/residual-connected. The
load-balance auxiliary loss is
`L_aux = alpha * N * sum_i f_i P_i`, where `f_i` is the fraction of tokens
dispatched to expert i and `P_i` is mean router probability for i. The paper
uses alpha near 1e-2 as a common setting, with sweeps. Router computation is
performed in float32 for stability; router initialization scale is reduced.

TRAINING REQUIRED:
Designed for end-to-end MoE training. Expert collapse/imbalance must be
managed during training.

INFERENCE MECHANISM:
Top-1 route with fixed capacity. This makes active compute predictable at
batch scale, but a single-token decode cannot exploit capacity balancing in
the same way as large-batch training.

MEMORY BEHAVIOR:
Only selected expert weights need to be active in principle, making the
mechanism attractive for SSD. Capacity buffers and expert metadata still
matter.

I/O BEHAVIOR:
Not studied. Token dropping is unacceptable for a quality-sensitive single
stream unless a fallback is guaranteed.

THROUGHPUT RESULTS:
Training scaling/throughput on TPU; no CPU/SSD single-token result.

QUALITY RESULTS:
The paper demonstrates competitive quality at scale, subject to routing
stability and capacity/drop settings.

HARDWARE:
TPUv3 training, large distributed batches.

WHAT ACTUALLY CAUSED THE SPEEDUP:
Top-1 conditional compute and reduced communication relative to dense scaling.

WHAT DID NOT WORK:
Router instability, expert imbalance/collapse and token overflow. Capacity
factor above one wastes memory/compute; too low drops tokens.

LIMITATIONS:
Single-token storage inference has no natural token pool to balance. An
auxiliary loss learned on batches does not guarantee a stable one-token route.

ASSUMPTIONS:
Large training batches and continued end-to-end optimization; overflow can
be tolerated or is rare.

TRANSFER TO QWEN3.8-27B:
Use router logits/top-k/aux-loss mechanics only for a trained branch. For
post-hoc conversion, a deterministic group predictor plus exact fallback is
safer than token dropping.

TRANSFER TO LLAMA.CPP:
Top-1 expert selection can map to a small number of SSD group reads, but
capacity-factor logic should not be copied blindly to batch-one decode.

WHAT WE SHOULD COPY:
[PRIOR ART] Float32 router logits, explicit balance diagnostics, capacity and
overflow accounting, and the distinction between top-k routing and capacity.

WHAT WE SHOULD NOT COPY:
[PRIOR ART LIMIT] Token dropping or batch-oriented load balance for a single
user unless a quality-preserving fallback exists.

OPEN QUESTIONS:
Could routing be constrained to storage-friendly contiguous groups without
destroying the learned function? Can a one-token stream use a small persistent
route history as its balancing signal?

[S11] EXPERT CHOICE ROUTING

TITLE: Mixture-of-Experts with Expert Choice Routing
AUTHORS: Zhou et al.
YEAR: 2022
VENUE: NeurIPS 2022
URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.09368 ; https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2202.09368
CODE: Paper method; no storage runtime.
RELEVANCE: medium-high

PROBLEM:
Avoid token-choice load imbalance and token dropping in MoE training.

KEY IDEA:
Experts choose their top-k tokens instead of each token choosing experts.
Each expert receives a fixed-capacity bucket, giving perfect expert capacity
balance and a variable number of experts per token.

ARCHITECTURE:
For an expert capacity factor, each expert selects tokens with highest router
affinity. A token can be selected by zero, one, or several experts; expert
capacity is fixed.

ALGORITHM:
The router creates an expert-by-token score matrix and top-k selection per
expert. It avoids the usual auxiliary balance loss. Reported token
distribution includes a majority with 1-2 experts, about 23% with 3-4 and
about 3% with more in one analysis. Capacity-factor 2 can have active work
similar to top-2 token routing.

IMPORTANT EQUATIONS:
The conceptual route is `I_e = TopK_tokens(score_e,:)`, with fixed `|I_e|`.
This reverses the usual `e = TopK_experts(score_token,:)` direction.

TRAINING REQUIRED:
End-to-end MoE training, large batches and TPU-scale experiments.

INFERENCE MECHANISM:
Variable experts/token. That can be useful when token difficulty varies, but
expert choice needs a population of tokens to fill buckets.

MEMORY BEHAVIOR:
Fixed expert buckets simplify training buffer allocation, not SSD single-token
reads.

I/O BEHAVIOR:
Not studied. For a single autoregressive token, expert-choice has too little
token population and can require awkward microbatch/history buffering.

THROUGHPUT RESULTS:
The paper reports over 2x convergence improvements in 8B/64E experiments and
large TPU training, not SSD inference.

QUALITY RESULTS:
Competitive quality and improved training efficiency under the evaluated
large-batch settings.

HARDWARE:
512 TPUv4-scale training.

WHAT ACTUALLY CAUSED THE SPEEDUP:
Balanced training batches and no token overflow, not lower single-token I/O.

WHAT DID NOT WORK:
The routing direction is a poor direct fit for one-token decode and variable
storage traffic.

LIMITATIONS:
No evidence for x86/SSD, no post-hoc dense conversion, and no deterministic
small active set for a single user.

ASSUMPTIONS:
Large batches can be formed and tokens can be assigned globally.

TRANSFER TO QWEN3.8-27B:
Useful only as a training/load-balance concept. A storage scheduler could
borrow fixed capacity buckets offline, but runtime route should remain
token-choice for single-stream decode.

TRANSFER TO LLAMA.CPP:
Not a direct runtime design.

WHAT WE SHOULD COPY:
[PRIOR ART] Fixed-capacity thinking and analysis of variable experts/token.

WHAT WE SHOULD NOT COPY:
[PRIOR ART LIMIT] Expert-choice dispatch for batch-one SSD fetches.

OPEN QUESTIONS:
Could a short rolling microbatch of speculative tokens make expert-choice
useful, or would KV/latency constraints outweigh it?

[S12] DYNAMOE

TITLE: From Dense to Dynamic: Token-Difficulty Driven MoEfication of Pre-Trained LLMs
AUTHORS: Nishu et al.
YEAR: 2025
VENUE: arXiv preprint
URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.12325 ; https://arxiv.org/html/2502.12325v1
CODE: No public storage/runtime implementation identified in the paper record.
RELEVANCE: high

PROBLEM:
Turn a dense pretrained LLM into a dynamic MoE whose compute depends on token
difficulty, using only limited additional training.

KEY IDEA:
Reorder MLP neurons by activation magnitude and create nested experts of
increasing width. A router predicts the smallest expert whose output is close
to the full dense MLP output. Attention is frozen.

ARCHITECTURE:
Only MLPs are changed. For E experts and dense width H, expert e has nested
width `H_e = floor((e+1)/E * H)`; the final expert is full width. The same
prefix rows/columns are used, so storage can naturally be prefix/range based.
The reported 4-expert sizes are 0.25H, 0.5H, 0.75H and H.

ALGORITHM:
Run all nested experts and the full dense output on calibration data. Define
similarity `S_{b,e}=<Y_e,Y_full>/<Y_full,Y_full>` and label each token with the
smallest e satisfying threshold theta. Train a two-linear-layer router
(hidden U=256) with about 33.6M parameters across layers, around 0.51% of the
base model. The loss combines language modeling and router terms, with one
reported `lambda_LLM=.2, lambda_router=1`.

TRAINING REQUIRED:
Reported continued fine-tuning uses about 10B Falcon RefinedWeb tokens,
AdamW lr1e-5. Without the router loss, the router can collapse to one expert
per layer. Neighboring expert misclassifications occur.

INFERENCE MECHANISM:
Predict token difficulty, select a nested width, and run prefix rows/columns.
This is attractive for storage because the selected data can be a contiguous
prefix, but it does not itself implement asynchronous SSD streaming.

MEMORY BEHAVIOR:
Nested experts duplicate/organize dense weights rather than materially
shrinking the full disk model. Prefix storage and a resident hot prefix are
possible.

I/O BEHAVIOR:
Not measured. No SSD bytes/token or cache/prefetch results.

THROUGHPUT RESULTS:
The paper explicitly does not provide a real deployment speed benchmark; it
reports compute/capacity and quality, including a claimed 1/9 Flextron cost
in its framing. This must not be treated as x86 wall-clock speed.

QUALITY RESULTS:
For Mistral7B, base average scores are about 77.8 on a 4-task group and 74.2
on a 7-task group. DynaMoE at theta .9 is about 74.0/70.1, theta .8 about
70.5/66.5 and theta .7 about 67.7/64.0, with parameter/compute points around
6.0/5.1/4.6B in the table. The quality cost is substantial at aggressive
sparsity.

HARDWARE:
Training/evaluation framework; no storage hardware result reported.

WHAT ACTUALLY CAUSED THE SPEEDUP:
Nested width reduction by token difficulty in the theoretical/compute sense.
No evidence yet of storage overlap or sparse CPU kernel speed.

WHAT DID NOT WORK:
Router loss removal caused collapse; neighboring width mistakes and threshold
tradeoffs limit quality; no end-to-end deployment result.

LIMITATIONS:
10B-token fine-tuning is not a cheap post-hoc conversion. Results are Mistral7B
and not Qwen3.8 gated hybrid blocks. It does not show SSD residency.

ASSUMPTIONS:
MLP output similarity is a good token-difficulty label; nested prefixes are
good approximations; attention can remain dense/frozen.

TRANSFER TO QWEN3.8-27B:
Borrow nested/prefix group layout and output-similarity labels. It may make
SSD reads sequential and cache-friendly. Expect a separate Qwen gated trace
and distillation/fine-tuning, and do not assume 10B tokens are acceptable.

TRANSFER TO LLAMA.CPP:
Prefix groups are easy to range-read and can use fixed slots. Router and
quality validation still need implementation.

WHAT WE SHOULD COPY:
[PRIOR ART] Nested groups; full-output similarity labels; explicit token-
difficulty route; router-loss anti-collapse test.

WHAT WE SHOULD NOT COPY:
[PRIOR ART LIMIT] Claimed compute cost as actual speed, or its quality/ratio
as a Qwen3.8 prediction.

OPEN QUESTIONS:
Can an output-aware group order be found for SwiGLU that preserves dense
quality at 20-40% width? Does nested prefix selection reduce random SSD I/O
enough to offset overfetch?

[S14] SVD CONTEXTUAL SPARSITY PREDICTORS

TITLE: SVD Contextual Sparsity Predictors for Fast LLM Inference
AUTHORS: Serbin, Koshkin, Sun, Bistrigova and Korikov
YEAR: 2026
VENUE: arXiv preprint
URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.14110 ; https://arxiv.org/html/2603.14110v1
CODE: No implementation repository verified during this pass.
RELEVANCE: high

PROBLEM:
Avoid training a large neural sparsity predictor while retaining contextual
FFN sparsity for edge inference.

KEY IDEA:
Approximate each gate projection with a data-whitened low-rank SVD, then add
per-neuron calibrated thresholds. The predictor is affine plus a Heaviside
mask and uses only a few thousand calibration tokens.

ARCHITECTURE:
The method modifies only ReGLU/dReLU FFNs. For
`FFN(x)=W_down(ReLU(W_gate x) * (W_up x))`, the mask is predicted from a
low-rank approximation of W_gate. Gate and up are executed sequentially:
predicted positives are checked by the real gate, then false positives are
removed before up/down.

ALGORITHM:
Given calibration X, form a Cholesky whitening S from `X X^T`, factor
`W_gate S = U Sigma V^T`, and use rank r:
`A = U_:,:r Sigma_:r,:r`, `B = V_:r,: S^-1`, predictor `P(x)=H(A B x+b)`.
Threshold/bias b is greedily calibrated. Importance for neuron i/sample t is
`w_i^t = |ReLU(gate_i) * up_i|^2 ||down_:,i||^2`; thresholds are increased by
the smallest empirical damage until a target sparsity/drop budget is reached.
The appendix gives a deterministic residual/error bound based on the SVD
residual and bias.

The sequential sparse-compute complexity is reported as
`r(d+D) + d(1-s)D + 2d(1-s')D`, versus dense `3dD`, where s is predicted and
s' realized sparsity. The authors explicitly warn that FLOP reduction does
not directly imply wall-clock speedup.

TRAINING REQUIRED:
No predictor gradient training; SVD, whitening and greedy threshold
calibration use a small calibration set. The base evaluated models are already
sparse/ReGLU/dReLU models, so this is not proof for unmodified Qwen SwiGLU.

INFERENCE MECHANISM:
Predict at the start of each FFN; compute gate; remove false positives; then
compute only remaining up/down. CUDA custom kernels and CANN executors are
provided in the paper's evaluation.

MEMORY BEHAVIOR:
Predictor storage is low-rank and per-neuron bias. No SSD working-set design.

I/O BEHAVIOR:
No flash results. Sequential gate/up is potentially useful for reducing cold
reads, but if gate itself must be fetched it still needs a storage schedule.

THROUGHPUT RESULTS:
Single RTX3090 GPU, fp16/vLLM, and Ascend 310P3. E2E includes prefill plus
200-token generation. On ProSparse-LLaMA2-7B, reported speedups are about
1.61-1.71x at 40-70% target sparsity; TurboSparse-Mistral about 1.67-1.84x;
SparseQwen2-7B about 1.78-1.86x. The best reported benchmark average drops
are under roughly 1.4 points at the high settings, with many under 1 point.

QUALITY RESULTS:
SparseQwen2-7B dense average 65.84; SVDP averages about 65.41/65.20/65.13/
64.49 at target s=.4/.5/.6/.7. The paper reports up to 1.8x E2E and less
than 1% degradation in selected complex math/code settings, but results use
already sparse/dReLU/ReGLU models.

HARDWARE:
RTX3090 and Ascend 310P3, CUDA/CANN, no x86 CPU or NVMe result.

WHAT ACTUALLY CAUSED THE SPEEDUP:
Low-rank predictor plus sequential gate validation and custom sparse kernels.

WHAT DID NOT WORK:
Naive SVD thresholding caused quality loss; whitening and bias calibration
were required. The paper notes predictor pipelining may be infeasible on some
hardware platforms.

LIMITATIONS:
ReGLU/ReLU/dReLU assumption; unmodified SiLU gate is not an exact zero mask;
GPU/NPU kernels; no storage. Predictor rank and calibration may be large for
Qwen's D=17408 if recall must be very high.

ASSUMPTIONS:
Gate projection is low-rank enough; calibrated scores generalize; exact gate
validation can safely filter false positives.

TRANSFER TO QWEN3.8-27B:
Test a Qwen-specific low-rank gate predictor as a training-light option, but
use continuous contribution thresholds, not binary ReLU correctness. A gated
sequential path may first read/predict gate groups, then fetch up/down only
for surviving groups. This could be especially useful for SSD bytes/token.

TRANSFER TO LLAMA.CPP:
The affine low-rank predictor is CPU-friendly in principle. Its value depends
on a sparse/group CPU kernel and storage-aware two-stage reads, neither of
which is provided.

WHAT WE SHOULD COPY:
[PRIOR ART] Data-aware low-rank gate predictor, per-neuron damage-aware
threshold calibration, sequential gate->up/down testing and explicit FLOP/
wall-clock distinction.

WHAT WE SHOULD NOT COPY:
[PRIOR ART LIMIT] ReGLU exact-zero guarantees or GPU speedups as Qwen SwiGLU
claims.

OPEN QUESTIONS:
Does a low-rank approximation predict Qwen3.8 gated contribution groups with
high recall? Is a two-stage SSD read cheaper than reading bundled gate+up at
once? Can the predictor be quantized and kept resident below 8 GB?

[S16] QWEN3.8-27B CHECKPOINT MAPPING

TITLE: Qwen3.8-27B model configuration and checkpoint index
AUTHORS: Qwen team
YEAR: 2026 checkpoint/configuration observed at research time
VENUE: Hugging Face model release
URL: https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.8-27B
     https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.8-27B/raw/main/config.json
     https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.8-27B/raw/main/model.safetensors.index.json
CODE: Model configuration/checkpoint tensor index
RELEVANCE: high

PROBLEM:
Establish the exact target architecture and tensor sizes before designing a
dense-to-sparse conversion.

KEY IDEA:
Qwen3.8-27B is not a conventional all-full-attention Transformer. It uses a
hybrid of gated linear-attention/DeltaNet-like blocks and full attention, with
gated dense FFNs in every layer.

ARCHITECTURE:
The observed config identifies `Qwen3_5ForConditionalGeneration`, text
`model_type=qwen3_5`, bf16 weights, hidden size 5120, intermediate size 17408,
64 hidden layers, `full_attention_interval=4`, 24 attention heads and 4 KV
heads, head dimension 256. The repeating layer types are 3 linear-attention
blocks followed by 1 full-attention block: 48 linear/hybrid and 16 full.
Linear attention fields include conv1d, A_log, dt_bias, in_proj_a/b/qkv/z,
linear key/value heads and output gate swish. Full attention has q/k/v/o and
q/k norms. Vocabulary size is 248320, max position 262144. MTP count is 1.
The release also includes a vision branch; text-only inference should not
load it.

PARAMETER ACCOUNTING:
Each MLP has three matrices of `17408 * 5120 = 89,128,960` parameters, or
267,386,880 parameters/layer. Across 64 layers that is 17.113B MLP params.
At bf16, one MLP is about 534.8 MB decimal (roughly 510 MiB); all MLPs are
about 34.23 GB decimal. A raw 4-bit payload is about 133.7 MB/layer and
8.55 GB all layers; adding group scales/biases and metadata gives a rough
q4 MLP pool near 9.6 GB. These are estimates, not a converted-file size.

[S18] NEUROPREFETCHER

TITLE: NeuroPrefetcher: Storage-Aware Sparse LLM Inference via Delta Prefetching
AUTHORS: Nobel Dhar et al.
YEAR: 2026
VENUE: ICPP 2026 paper / arXiv preprint
URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.22643 ; https://arxiv.org/html/2608.22643v1
     https://doi.org/10.1145/3832810.3832862
CODE / ARTIFACT: https://github.com/nobeldhar/llm-activation-sparsity
RELEVANCE: very high

PROBLEM:
Run a model larger than resident memory when dynamic sparse MLP weights must
come from NVMe during autoregressive decode.

KEY IDEA:
Predict all downstream MLP gate/up active masks with one resident predictor,
cluster masks into a centroid vocabulary, store neuron records contiguously,
and fetch only mask deltas between adjacent tokens. Reuse fixed slots instead
of compacting the entire working set each token.

ARCHITECTURE:
Jetson AGX Orin 32 GB unified LPDDR5, 12 Arm A78AE cores, Ampere GPU and
Samsung 990 Pro NVMe. Mistral7B is about 7.24B parameters/13.49 GiB fp16,
32 layers, hidden4096/intermediate14336. A dense prefix fits in the cap;
the sparse suffix has active row buffers. Embeddings/head/final norm,
attention/projections, per-layer norms, predictor and centroid data stay
resident. The paper says layers 1-31 are predicted; its dense/sparse handling
of down is described in a way that should be checked carefully because the
main layout and delta text both discuss active down-transposed columns.

ALGORITHM:
Threshold small activation values per layer and cluster binary gate/up masks
into K=512 centroids. The predictor uses token embedding, pre-attention norm,
a 128-dimensional attention sketch, post-attention norm scalar and layer-0
gate centroid ID. It has four feature projection blocks to width 512, a
centroid embedding, a four-block residual MLP trunk and gate/up heads for each
later layer. It is about 206.8M parameters / 413.6 MB fp16, 2.86% of base.

For current active set A_t and previous A_(t-1):
`D_in = A_t - A_(t-1)`
`D_out = A_(t-1) - A_t`.
Only D_in is read; D_out slots are reusable. A fixed Kmax slot map avoids
compaction. The first token requires a full predicted set.

Storage records are repacked gate/up/downT for each intermediate neuron,
approximately 24.5 KiB per fp16 Mistral neuron record (3*4096*2 bytes plus
alignment in the described layout). Active IDs are sorted and adjacent ranges
coalesced. Linux io_uring with O_DIRECT, QD256, page-aligned registered/mapped
staging and a fused deinterleave/scatter kernel are used. Some staging/cache
coherence is Jetson-specific.

IMPORTANT EQUATIONS:
Delta traffic is proportional to `|D_in| * bytes_per_neuron`, not
`|A_t| * bytes_per_neuron`, when reuse holds. Their mask thresholds and
centroid/predictor equations are implementation-specific; the important
system equation is the delta set above.

TRAINING REQUIRED:
Mask clustering and predictor training; the predictor is 2.86% of base in
the reported setup. The model itself is an existing sparse-activation setup,
not a dense Qwen3.8 post-hoc conversion.

INFERENCE MECHANISM:
Predict future masks from a single resident predictor, issue async O_DIRECT
delta reads, scatter into fixed buffers, then execute sparse MLP. A dense
prefix is used to reduce storage exposure under a memory cap.

MEMORY BEHAVIOR:
Evaluates caps around 9-17 GiB and reports a substantial dense prefix plus
sparse suffix. At 9 GB, predictor and fixed resident state consume a large
fraction; active working buffers are bounded.

I/O BEHAVIOR:
At 14.8 GiB, NVMe reads are about 103 MiB/token; at 9 GiB, just under 1 GiB/
token. 80-87% of latency is NVMe in reported cap settings. Active-mask
persistence is about 82-85% token-to-token, making delta reuse the dominant
I/O reduction.

THROUGHPUT RESULTS:
At a 14 GiB-ish cap, NeuroPrefetcher is reported around 3.22 tok/s versus
llama.cpp about 0.34 tok/s in the model-exceeds-memory regime. At 9 GiB,
about 1.7 tok/s; at 13.2 GiB, about 3.7 tok/s. When llama.cpp also fits near
13.9/14.8 GiB, its throughput is roughly 5.9/7.7 tok/s and the advantage
narrows to about 1.2/1.1x. This is far below a universal 10-20 tok/s target.

QUALITY RESULTS:
The abstract reports preserving roughly 92-96% of dense accuracy; evaluation
uses WikiText2 perplexity and 10-shot HellaSwag on Llama3-8B/related models.
Centroid/predictor layer decisions are reported around 99% accuracy, but
quality depends on threshold/clustering. It is not Qwen3.8 quality evidence.

HARDWARE:
Jetson AGX Orin/Arm/NVIDIA unified memory, Samsung 990 Pro NVMe, CUDA/IO
Uring/O_DIRECT. Not CPU-only x86.

WHAT ACTUALLY CAUSED THE SPEEDUP:
Delta prefetch, 82-85% active-set reuse, contiguous neuron records, fixed
slots, O_DIRECT/queueing, fused scatter, and dense-prefix selection. Predictor
overhead is secondary; NVMe remains 80-87% of latency.

WHAT DID NOT WORK:
At low caps, active-set deltas become large and I/O dominates. The method
does not reach 10-20 tok/s. Platform-specific mapped staging/coherence and
GPU kernels do not transfer directly to CPU.

LIMITATIONS:
Mistral/Llama-like model, Jetson GPU/unified-memory hardware, no dense Qwen3.8
conversion, no <8 GB x86 result. The paper's handling of down matrices should
be replicated exactly before using its bytes/neuron calculation.

ASSUMPTIONS:
Activation masks persist across tokens; centroids generalize; contiguous
neuron records and O_DIRECT queues outperform page cache; a dense prefix can
fit.

TRANSFER TO QWEN3.8-27B:
This is the strongest direct system template: centroid or low-rank mask
predictor, one early predictor, delta reads, fixed slots, coalesced ranges,
O_DIRECT/io_uring experiment, and dense prefix. Qwen requires a new gated
contribution threshold, 64-layer/hybrid mapping and x86 CPU kernels. Its
reported bytes/token give a realistic warning: even excellent reuse may not
meet 10-20 tok/s under 8 GB.

TRANSFER TO LLAMA.CPP:
The layout/scheduler ideas transfer conceptually. Linux io_uring/O_DIRECT can
be tested, but C++ CPU staging and quantized deinterleave kernels replace
CUDA/Jetson paths. Exact read histograms must be added.

WHAT WE SHOULD COPY:
[PRIOR ART] Delta sets; fixed slot map; mask clustering; one lookahead
predictor; dense prefix; sorted range coalescing; O_DIRECT microbenchmarks;
read bytes/token and latency fractions.

WHAT WE SHOULD NOT COPY:
[PRIOR ART LIMIT] Jetson throughput, predictor size, activation thresholds,
or GPU staging as Qwen/x86 guarantees. Do not claim delta prefetch itself as
novel.

OPEN QUESTIONS:
Will Qwen3.8 gated group masks persist 82-85% across decode? Can 4-bit group
records reduce the 24.5 KiB/neuron equivalent enough? Can CPU execution and
NVMe overlap without a GPU scheduler? What dense-prefix size leaves useful
cache under 8 GB?

GGUF / LLAMA.CPP MAPPING

Current qwen35.cpp creates dense `ffn_gate`, `ffn_up`, `ffn_down` tensors and
uses a parallel SiLU FFN. The generic semantic mapping corresponds to names
like `blk.{i}.ffn_gate.weight`, `blk.{i}.ffn_up.weight` and
`blk.{i}.ffn_down.weight`, but a converted file must be inspected because
GGUF names are generated through tensor enums and mapping tables.

Candidate grouping must account for actual matrix orientation. Semantically:
gate/up have one row per intermediate neuron in HF; down has one aligned
column per intermediate neuron. In GGML layout/source shape the dimensions
are represented as `{n_embd,n_ff}` for gate/up and `{n_ff,n_embd}` for down.
The converter should move/pack all three with one permutation and record
strides/offsets explicitly.

CANDIDATE SPARSIFICATION POINTS

ATTENTION:
possible? Yes, contextual head/key/value sparsity exists in DejaVu.
risk? High for Qwen3.8: 48 linear-state blocks and 16 full-attention blocks
have different semantics; sparse state updates can create accumulated error;
KV/head misses can require recomputation. Keep dense initially.

FFN GATE PROJECTION:
possible? Yes. It is the natural early predictor/filter and can be grouped.
risk? A predictor false negative changes the final gated contribution; gate
alone is not the output importance because up and down matter.

FFN UP PROJECTION:
possible? Yes, after a gate/group mask. It must share intermediate indices.
risk? The up value is not naturally zero under SiLU; skipping small values is
an approximation, not exact ReLU sparsity.

FFN DOWN PROJECTION:
possible? Yes, select aligned down columns or down-transposed rows.
risk? Random column access and quantization layout can destroy CPU/SSD
locality; repacking is likely necessary.

WHOLE FFN EXPERTS:
possible? Yes, use MoEfication/DynaMoE-style aligned groups.
risk? Group granularity creates overfetch and group error; a conventional
router adds training/symmetry issues and does not guarantee dense quality.

NEURON CLUSTERS:
possible? Yes, likely best first unit for storage.
risk? Single-neuron records are too small for SSD; large clusters reduce
selectivity. Need contribution-aware clustering and contiguous packing.

LAYERS:
possible? Full layer paging is possible, but a 64-layer decode would read
large blocks and lose fine-grained reuse.
risk? Layer skip changes the architecture severely and leaves no good
lookahead; avoid as first path.

MTP:
possible? Could support speculative decoding.
risk? Extra resident state/compute and a second unvalidated source of error;
not useful until basic sparse decode works.

RESIDENT FLOOR ESTIMATE

Keep initially: embeddings/head, all 64 layer norms, 48 linear-attention
branches, 16 full-attention branches, routers/predictor, runtime, staging and
KV. The exact q4 floor is not yet measured. A conservative planning range is
roughly 5-7 GB for non-MLP weights plus runtime/KV depending quantization and
context. If true, an 8 GB machine leaves only approximately 1-3 GB for the
active MLP cache and staging. This is a hypothesis and must be measured from
an actual converted file.

5. CANDIDATE ARCHITECTURES
------------------------------------------------------------

All estimates below are ranges for the Qwen3.8 target, not measurements. The
model has 17.1B MLP parameters; the non-MLP resident floor is a major unknown.
Use q4 raw payload plus scales/metadata for storage estimates.

ARCHITECTURE A - MOEFICATION-STYLE GATED GROUPS

Method: Permute intermediate neurons by gated coactivation/contribution,
partition into groups (candidate 128-512 neurons), and predict top groups.

active parameters/token: approximately 10-30% of MLP if traces permit;
                         whole-model active parameter floor remains high
resident parameters:     non-MLP floor + hot groups/cache
SSD bytes/token:         roughly fresh-active-groups * 2-3 MB/group/layer;
                         target must be <=200-400 MB/token at 20-10 tok/s
random reads/token:      must be coalesced to tens/hundreds of ranges, not
                         one read per neuron
compute/token:            10-30% of MLP arithmetic plus predictor/gather;
                         dense hybrid/attention remains
predictor cost:           small MLP or SVD/group predictor; must be early
likely quality loss:      unknown for SiLU; expect measurable truncation loss
required training:        calibration; possibly distillation/fine-tuning
implementation difficulty:high; quantized down-column packing and CPU kernel

ARCHITECTURE B - DEJAVU-LIKE CONTEXTUAL GROUPS

Method: Leave dense weights semantically intact; predictor emits per-layer
group masks and sparse kernels execute selected groups.

active parameters/token: 15-30% MLP as an initial target, not a claim
resident parameters:     dense non-MLP + predictor + hot groups
SSD bytes/token:         depends on mask delta; unacceptable if each layer
                         reads full 15-30% independently
random reads/token:      poor unless ranges are sorted/coalesced
compute/token:            sparse MLP plus predictor; attention dense
predictor cost:           per-layer predictors or one early predictor
likely quality loss:      potentially low at conservative recall; gated tests
                         required
required training:        predictor labels; no model retraining initially
implementation difficulty:high CPU sparse kernel and lookahead scheduler

ARCHITECTURE C - HOT RESIDENT CORE + COLD SSD GROUPS

Method: Keep globally hot groups resident, predict context-dependent cold
groups, and stream only cold misses.

active parameters/token: 40-70% of MLP may be touched if hot coverage is good;
                         arithmetic reduction is smaller but robust
resident parameters:     non-MLP + hot core, perhaps 20-50% MLP
SSD bytes/token:         hot coverage *must* reduce misses below 200-400 MB
random reads/token:      low if hot/cold records are clustered
compute/token:            still high; likely a safer CPU path
predictor cost:           low/medium online predictor
likely quality loss:      low if all required cold groups are fetched
required training:        profiling; predictor calibration
implementation difficulty:medium-high; cache policy is central

ARCHITECTURE D - SPARSE UPCYCLED / VIRTUAL-GROUP MOE

Method: Copy dense weights into narrower/replicated experts, train a router,
and continue pretraining/distill.

active parameters/token: 25-50% of expanded capacity depending top-k/width;
                         not necessarily below original dense MLP
resident parameters:     router + hot experts + non-MLP
SSD bytes/token:         group/expert misses; layout can be sequential
random reads/token:      potentially large if many fine experts
compute/token:            controlled by top-k/width but training needed
predictor cost:           conventional router, low enough but route quality
likely quality loss:      unknown; DynaMoE shows aggressive quality loss
required training:        substantial continued training; possibly billions
implementation difficulty:high; checkpoint conversion + training + runtime

ARCHITECTURE E - SVD / GATE-FIRST SEQUENTIAL FILTER

Method: Build a low-rank predictor of gate rows, validate gate values, then
fetch/run only surviving up/down groups.

active parameters/token: predicted mask can be 30-60% sparse, realized higher
resident parameters:     non-MLP + low-rank predictor + gate metadata
SSD bytes/token:         gate prediction/data first, then surviving up/down;
                         two-stage reads may save bytes but add latency
random reads/token:      coalesce group ranges; do not read per row
compute/token:            predictor + gate + sparse up/down
predictor cost:           `r(d+D)` per layer or one shared predictor; Qwen D
                         is 17408, so this is nontrivial
likely quality loss:      unknown for SiLU; SVDP ReGLU results encouraging
required training:        calibration only initially
implementation difficulty:high; sequential CPU/SSD overlap is unresolved

ARCHITECTURE F - HYBRID RECOMMENDED HYPOTHESIS

Method: Optional static block/N:M reduction, contribution-aware Qwen gated
groups, resident hot core, one early predictor, delta prefetch, fixed staging,
and optional distillation only after trace validation.

active parameters/token: target 20-40% MLP initially; measure rather than set
resident parameters:     non-MLP floor + hot core + predictor + 0.5-2 GB cache
SSD bytes/token:         kill if sustained fresh traffic exceeds target budget;
                         aim <100-200 MB/token for 20 tok/s aspiration
random reads/token:      sorted/coalesced group ranges; target low tens
compute/token:            hybrid dense floor + 20-40% MLP + CPU bookkeeping
predictor cost:           one early group predictor, perhaps centroid/SVD
likely quality loss:      first safe target <1% local KL / small PPL shift;
                         benchmark loss unknown
required training:        calibration first; small distillation next; no
                         pretraining-from-scratch assumption
implementation difficulty:very high; only candidate aligned with question

BACK-OF-ENVELOPE STORAGE BUDGET

At 4 GB/s effective sequential storage bandwidth:
`4 GB/s / 10 token/s = 400 MB/token`.
`4 GB/s / 20 token/s = 200 MB/token`.

These are ceilings. If effective random/coalesced throughput is 1.25 GB/s,
the corresponding budgets are 125 MB/token at 10 tok/s and 62.5 MB/token at
20 tok/s. If a q4 Qwen MLP layer is roughly 150 MB and a group of 256 neurons
is about 2.0-2.5 MB including metadata, then 64 layers with 10% fresh groups
could approach 1 GB/token before delta reuse. That is impossible at 10-20
tok/s unless the active sets persist and the cache covers most data.

CONSEQUENCE: The first experiment must measure active-set overlap and bytes
after a realistic layout. An “active 10%” result without delta/read accounting
does not support the architecture.

7. FAILURE MODES
------------------------------------------------------------

- Expert/group prediction misses: false negatives either force synchronous
  random reads, use an approximation, or corrupt quality. False positives
  waste bytes and cache slots. Record both separately.
- Random I/O amplification: noncontiguous down columns or single-neuron reads
  can make “sparse” traffic slower than sequential dense reads.
- Small-read SSD collapse: align records and benchmark 4 KiB, 16 KiB, 32 KiB,
  128 KiB and multi-range requests on the actual drive.
- Cache thrashing: a global cache can help cross-layer reuse but one hot
  sequence can evict another layer's working set. Track per-layer and global
  hit rates.
- Activation distribution shifts: prompt calibration may not represent long
  autoregressive decode, code, multilingual prompts or rare reasoning tokens.
- Activation shift after sparsification: a small MLP error changes the next
  layer's routing/active set, so local error can compound.
- Router collapse/expert symmetry: copied experts remain identical without
  enough training; router loss can collapse to one group.
- Quality loss from SiLU approximation: unlike ReLU, omitted negative/small
  values are not exact zeros. Down projection norms alone are insufficient.
- Dense floor dominance: 48 linear-attention blocks, 16 full-attention blocks,
  embeddings, norms and output head can dominate after MLP pruning.
- Output embedding/head dominance: the untied 1.27B-parameter head is a
  resident compute/memory cost and may erase the expected speedup.
- Quantization interactions: group scales/biases, dequantization and row/column
  repacking can alter contribution rankings and add metadata overhead.
- CPU sparse-kernel overhead: gathers, sorting, branchy loops and small GEMMs
  can cost more than skipped multiplies; BLAS dense kernels may win at modest
  sparsity.
- Bookkeeping overhead: route prediction, slot maps, locks, futures and
  synchronization can dominate one-token decode.
- Page-cache interference: mmap can evict resident model/KV pages; `WILLNEED`
  is advisory, and accidental page faults can serialize execution.
- Linux readahead mismatch: sequential readahead can help ranges or read too
  much; O_DIRECT may be necessary but increases alignment/implementation work.
- mmap accidentally becoming resident: virtual address size is not RAM use;
  measure RSS/PSS and major faults, not mapped bytes.
- SSD endurance and energy: high reads/token can consume energy and device
  lifetime even if latency is overlapped. See [S15].
- Preprocessing/layout cost: creating grouped q4 records, changing down
  orientation, storing indexes and validating tensor alignment can be a large
  conversion project.
- Predictor storage: NeuroPrefetcher shows a predictor can consume hundreds
  of MB; under 8 GB this is material.
- Prefill/decode mismatch: a full-layer prefill path may be optimal while
  token decode needs delta groups. Do not use prompt throughput as decode.
- KV-cache growth: long context consumes RAM and reduces active group cache.
- Hybrid-state error: sparse MLP errors enter linear-attention recurrent state
  and may persist longer than in ordinary full-attention blocks.
- Tooling mismatch: MLX, CUDA, CANN, Android and Jetson synchronization do
  not prove x86/llama.cpp behavior.
- Token capacity/dropping: Switch-style overflow is unacceptable unless a
  dense/exact fallback prevents silent quality loss.

8. IMPLEMENTATION IDEAS
------------------------------------------------------------

Do not implement these yet. Each item is labeled by provenance.

[PRIOR ART] Build an activation-trace tool that records per-layer gate, up,
down contribution, selected group IDs, group byte ranges, active-set overlap,
and local/full-model output error. Inspired by MoEfication [S02], SVDP [S14]
and NeuroPrefetcher [S18].

[PRIOR ART] Preserve the same intermediate permutation across gate/up/down and
store aligned records. From MoEfication [S02], Flash [S03], PowerInfer-2 [S04]
and NeuroPrefetcher [S18].

[OUR HYPOTHESIS] Use contribution score
`q_i=|silu(gate_i)*up_i|^2 * ||down[:,i]||^2` and a group score that includes
within-group contribution correlation. This is a Qwen-specific extension;
validate against exact output error.

[PRIOR ART] Keep a global bounded LRU plus a separate prefetch buffer and
fixed staging slots. From Edge0 [S01].

[PRIOR ART] Use one early predictor with a one-layer/multi-layer horizon and
delta sets rather than per-layer-late prediction. From Edge0 [S01], DejaVu
[S07] and NeuroPrefetcher [S18].

[OUR HYPOTHESIS] Use a Qwen-specific low-rank/SVD gate predictor as a training-
light alternative, with per-group damage-aware thresholds and optional
gate-first reads. Inspired by SVDP [S14], but Qwen uses SiLU and needs new
validation.

[PRIOR ART] Store gate/up/down group data in sorted contiguous ranges; consider
O_DIRECT/io_uring, aligned staging and fixed slot maps. From Flash [S03] and
NeuroPrefetcher [S18].

[OUR HYPOTHESIS] For llama.cpp, introduce a separate sparse-FFN tensor family
and operator rather than making ordinary GGUF mmap tensors page-fault into the
working set. Store an index table with layer/group offsets, quantization
metadata, and aligned gate/up/down strides.

[PRIOR ART] Retain globally hot groups in RAM and stream context-dependent
cold groups. From PowerInfer [S08] and PowerInfer-2 [S04].

[MODIFICATION] Make the hot score a combination of global frequency, expected
SSD read cost, contribution norm and predicted next-token probability, rather
than frequency alone.

[PRIOR ART] Use full-layer reads during prefill and sparse/delta reads during
decode. From Edge0 [S01].

[MODIFICATION] Keep all Qwen3.8 attention/linear tensors on the dense path for
the first experiment; add attention sparsity only after MLP-only traces pass.

[OUR HYPOTHESIS] Optional SparseGPT-style static block/N:M reduction can lower
the SSD pool before contextual selection, but only if applied consistently to
the gated groups and measured separately from dynamic sparsity.

[PRIOR ART] Record exact cache hits, wasted prefetch, reads/token, range-size
histogram, major faults, queue depth, overlap and stall time. Edge0 exposes
several counters; Flash/NeuroPrefetcher show why these measurements matter.

[MODIFICATION] Define emergency miss policy: first try a bounded synchronous
read; if latency exceeds a threshold, use a quality-preserving dense fallback
or conservative resident group, never silent zero/drop in quality benchmarks.

[OUR HYPOTHESIS] Distill the sparse model to the dense Qwen3.8 logits after
the trace chooses a target group budget. Only then consider a router or
virtual-group upcycling. This avoids spending training effort on an impossible
SSD byte budget.

9. EXPERIMENTS WE MUST RUN
------------------------------------------------------------

EXPERIMENT 1 - GATED QWEN ACTIVE-SET / ERROR TRACE (FIRST IMPLEMENTATION)

hypothesis:
Qwen3.8 gated MLP contributions can be grouped so that a substantial fraction
of MLP rows can be omitted per token, with high token-to-token overlap and a
storage traffic budget compatible with a future 10-20 tok/s design.

model:
Qwen3.8-27B text-only checkpoint if feasible. If 27B cannot fit for tracing,
use a smaller Qwen3.5/Qwen-family gated model only as a pipeline proxy and
label all results as proxy. Trace full dense outputs first; do not modify
weights or begin a sparse implementation.

hardware:
Available Linux/x86 machine; record CPU, RAM, NVMe model, filesystem,
quantization, threads. A GPU is acceptable for trace generation but the
trace must be replayable against x86 storage assumptions.

metric:
Per-layer and whole-model next-token KL/logit error; MLP output relative L2;
active fraction at multiple thresholds; group truncation error; gate/up/down
contribution; Jaccard overlap across adjacent tokens; contiguous range count;
fresh group bytes/token under candidate group sizes 64/128/256/512; prompt vs
decode split; CPU trace time.

success threshold:
At least one operating point with >=50% MLP activity omitted (or a clearly
measured lower bound for unmodified SiLU), <=1% local calibration perplexity
or small agreed KL increase, >=80% adjacent active-set persistence, and
estimated coalesced fresh reads <=200-400 MB/token before overlap optimism.

kill threshold:
No point below 30% omitted activity at acceptable error; adjacent overlap
below 60%; or every acceptable-quality point requires >400 MB/token at an
effective 4 GB/s device. If the resident floor alone exceeds 8 GB, stop the
8 GB objective or reduce model scope before building a runtime.

EXPERIMENT 2 - STORAGE LAYOUT MICROBENCHMARK

hypothesis:
Aligned group records and sorted range reads make the selected Qwen groups
fast enough; per-neuron/page-fault access does not.

model:
Synthetic records with exact Qwen3.8 q4 dimensions plus a small real converted
layer. Compare group 64/128/256/512 and gate+up+down layouts.

hardware:
Target Linux/x86 NVMe; report filesystem, direct/buffered I/O, queue depth,
threads, read alignment and thermals.

metric:
MiB/s, p50/p95/p99 read latency, IOPS, CPU copy/dequant time, ranges/request,
bytes overfetched, queue depth, read amplification and energy if available.

success threshold:
Sustained candidate delta reads at >=50% of the device's realistic sequential
bandwidth with bounded p99; group reads materially beat one-neuron/page reads.

kill threshold:
Small/range reads remain below the bytes/token budget even with full overlap,
or CPU dequant/gather dominates the dense baseline.

EXPERIMENT 3 - EXACT REPLAY CACHE/PREFETCH SIMULATOR

hypothesis:
Edge0/NeuroPrefetcher-style global cache, fixed slots and delta prefetch can
keep cold reads within budget using the actual Qwen trace.

model: Experiment 1 traces; no model execution needed.
hardware: Parameterized by measured NVMe/CPU results; then real machine.
metric: cache hit/miss, wasted prefetch, fresh bytes/token, ranges/token,
lookahead misses, slot churn, queue occupancy, hypothetical stall time.
success threshold: >=80% active-set reuse, >=90% predicted-group recall,
fresh bytes within target at p95, no pathological per-layer thrashing.
kill threshold: p95/prompt shifts cause >400 MB/token, predictor recall below
99% for the groups that matter, or cache needs more RAM than available.

EXPERIMENT 4 - CPU SPARSE FFN VS DENSE BASELINE

hypothesis:
At the trace-selected group sizes, CPU sparse execution plus predictor/gather
is faster than dense q4 FFN, not just fewer FLOPs.

model: one or several Qwen3.8 MLP layers, then full text decode.
hardware: target x86 CPU, one thread count sweep, RAM under target cap.
metric: layer time, decode tok/s, predictor time, gather/dequant time, branch
misses/cache misses, dense-vs-sparse crossover by active fraction.
success threshold: measured layer and end-to-end speedup at the selected point;
initially require >=1.2x end-to-end before any SSD complexity.
kill threshold: dense q4 wins at all acceptable sparsity points or sparse
bookkeeping consumes the expected savings.

EXPERIMENT 5 - QUALITY RECOVERY

hypothesis:
Small calibration/distillation/fine-tuning recovers most of the gated group
truncation loss without changing the bytes budget.

model: Qwen3.8 target or proxy, group layout from Experiment 1.
hardware: available training/inference hardware; record tokens and time.
metric: calibration KL/PPL, WikiText2, HellaSwag and selected math/code tasks;
long-context recurrent/hybrid-state drift; active bytes unchanged.
success threshold: <=1-2% benchmark degradation at the selected IO budget,
with a reproducible finite training budget.
kill threshold: quality recovery requires full pretraining-scale data or
increases active bytes enough to invalidate storage.

EXPERIMENT 6 - END-TO-END STORAGE-RESIDENT DECODE

hypothesis:
The integrated design reaches useful tokens/s under 8 GB without silent
quality fallback.

model: final chosen Qwen3.8 sparse representation.
hardware: Linux/x86, <=8 GB RSS cap, target NVMe, single stream, context and
decode lengths reported separately.

metric: tok/s prefill/decode, p50/p95 token latency, RSS/PSS, major faults,
bytes read/token, read ranges, SSD utilization, CPU utilization, cache/predictor
stats, benchmark quality.

success threshold:
At least 10 tok/s decode with <=8 GB RSS would meet the ambitious target; a
lower reproducible point with a clear bottleneck is still scientifically useful.

kill threshold:
Below 5 tok/s at the most favorable safe quality point, >8 GB RSS, or
quality fallback/misses that cannot be bounded. Do not continue to optimize a
design that fails the first three experiments.
