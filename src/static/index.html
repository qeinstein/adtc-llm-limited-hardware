The problem

Community health workers are often the only medical presence for a village — and the front line of care for childhood fever, malaria, pregnancy danger signs, and more. Cloud AI tools are a non-starter here: unreliable connectivity, no budget for subscriptions in USD, and real privacy concerns for patient data. Whatever helps has to run entirely on the device already in the room.

"A CHW needs fast, trustworthy guidance in the language the patient speaks — not a chatbot guessing, and not something that stops working the moment the signal drops."

Our approach

Rather than assume "bigger model is better," we worked backward from exactly how this challenge is scored — and let the real hardware constraints, not intuition, decide the design.

1

A small model, chosen with real numbers

We measured actual speed, memory, and accuracy across several model sizes on hardware matching the real evaluation machine — and found a small, efficient model wins decisively once you account for how heavily the scoring rewards speed and low memory use.

2

Grounded in real clinical guidance

Every answer is checked against a curated bank of WHO/IMCI-based clinical guidelines before the model responds — so recommendations are traceable to real medical protocol, not just the model's memory.

3

Trained for how it's actually tested

Instead of generic fine-tuning, we trained the model specifically to correctly rank the right answer against wrong ones — matching the exact way the model gets evaluated, not just "sound plausible."

4

Genuinely bilingual, safely

We expanded our training data into Kiswahili by having a larger AI model elaborate strictly on our own vetted clinical guidelines — never inventing new medical claims, only translating and rephrasing what was already verified.

What we learned along the way

A few honest highlights from actually building this — including the mistakes we caught before they became real problems.

Discovery

The official test machine is deliberately slowed down

We found that the real grading environment disables the CPU speed features most laptops have on by default — a detail we suspect many teams will miss entirely. We built our whole testing process around matching that exact environment, instead of trusting numbers from a normal fast laptop.

Course correction

Our first instinct on model size was wrong

The inherited starting point aimed for the largest model that could technically fit in memory. Running the actual scoring math against real hardware measurements showed this would have thrown away most of the available score — so we rebuilt around a much smaller, faster model instead, and let head-to-head testing (not assumption) pick the final compression level too.

Bug caught by testing

A silent scoring bug that would have produced meaningless numbers

Early on, our accuracy-checking tool reported results no better than random guessing. Rather than accept that as "the model is bad," we tested the checker itself against an obvious question — and found the checker was reading the model's confidence scores incorrectly. Fixed, it immediately produced sensible results. A good reminder to distrust your own tools until you've verified them.

Real-world debugging

A memory crash traced to one model's unusually large vocabulary

A training optimization that should have been safe caused an out-of-memory crash. Tracing it down, the cause was specific to this model having a much larger vocabulary than typical — an easy detail to miss, and a good example of why "should work" isn't the same as "verified to work."

Iteration

Rebuilding the training process twice — for speed, never for correctness

We restructured how training examples are processed twice to cut real training time significantly, and mathematically verified each change produced identical results before trusting it — genuine speed gains, with zero compromise on what the model was actually learning.
