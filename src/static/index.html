The problem

Community health workers are often the only medical presence for a village.

They are the front line for childhood fever, malaria, pregnancy danger signs and injury. Cloud AI is a non-starter: connectivity is unreliable, subscriptions are priced in dollars, and patient data should not leave the clinic. Whatever helps has to run on the device already in the room.

A health worker needs trustworthy guidance in the language the patient speaks — not a chatbot guessing, and not something that stops working when the signal drops.

Approach

We worked backwards from how the system is actually measured.

Rather than assume a bigger model is better, every significant decision here was settled by measurement on hardware matching the real evaluation machine.

  1. A big model that fits in small RAM

    A 35-billion-parameter sparse mixture-of-experts where only ~3B parameters work on any token. Narrow K4/16 routing plus bounded expert staging keeps the working set under 3 GB on a CPU-only laptop — the capability stays, the RAM bill does not.

  2. Grounded in real clinical guidance

    Answers are retrieved from a curated bank of WHO/IMCI-based guidelines before the model responds, so recommendations trace back to real protocol rather than model memory.

  3. Adapted without retraining

    The weights are not fine-tuned. Instead: a clinical system prompt, 18 domains of structured guidance, deterministic safety rules, and output linting — all inspectable, all versioned, none of it baked opaquely into parameters.

  4. Bilingual, without inventing medicine

    English and Kiswahili end to end, with repetition guards for the failure shapes real testing found. Authority claims (WHO, IMCI, NCDC) must come from retrieved guidance or they are removed.

Engineering notes

What went wrong, and what it taught us.

The useful parts of a build log are the mistakes. These are the ones that changed the design.

Discovery

The evaluation machine deliberately disables the CPU acceleration most laptops rely on. We rebuilt our entire testing process around that environment rather than trusting numbers from a fast development machine — a detail easy to miss, and one that invalidates optimistic benchmarks.

Correction

Our first instinct — route every token to all 8 experts — moved gigabytes per answer and blew the RAM budget. Measuring each expert's contribution showed narrow K4/16 routing keeps the answers while fitting the budget, so the sparse executor stages only what each token actually needs.

Bug caught by testing

Our accuracy checker once reported results no better than random. Rather than accept "the model is bad," we tested the checker itself against a question with an obvious answer and found it was reading the model's confidence scores incorrectly. Distrust your instruments until you have verified them.

Safety

Retrieval returned the two correct guidelines for a pre-eclampsia case, then padded the context with a loosely-related third about diabetes — and the model answered about blood sugar. A weak match is worse than no match, because the model cannot tell which part of its context to ignore. Weak matches are now discarded.

Root cause

An early evaluation came back with empty answers — not wrong ones. The model had spent its whole token budget thinking and never reached a reply. Reasoning now gets its own allowance with a protected, guaranteed answer budget, and urgent safety verdicts render before reasoning finishes.
